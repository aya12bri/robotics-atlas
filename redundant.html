Self-motion: the arm moves, the tool does not.

Seven revolute joints, like a human arm: shoulder (3), elbow (1), wrist (3). To place and orient a tool in space you need 6; the 7th is one too many on purpose. For every pose of the tool there are infinitely many postures of the arm, so the robot can move its elbow out of the way, avoid a joint limit or a singularity, or let a person push it by hand, while the tool keeps working. The geometry here is the KUKA LBR iiwa 7 R800.

Joints

Move a slider, or type a value in a number box. With q = 0 for all joints, the arm points straight up.

Kinematic diagram (schéma cinématique)

Frames:

A circle is a joint whose axis points at you (⊙) or away from you (⊗); a bar is a joint whose axis lies in the picture. The joints alternate: a rotation about the arm (q1, q3, q5, q7), then a bend (q2, q4, q6). Axes: x red, y green, z blue. Choose “Frame {i} only” to read one frame at a time.

DH table (standard)

Frame i is attached to the axis of joint i+1. Each row is: Ai = Rz(θ) · Tz(d) · Tx(a) · Rx(α). All the a are zero: the axes cross two by two, which gives a spherical shoulder (q1, q2, q3 meet at one point) and a spherical wrist (q5, q6, q7). Orange numbers are the current values.

DHM table (modified DH)

Frame i is attached to the axis of joint i. Each row is: Ai = Rx(αi−1) · Tx(ai−1) · Rz(θ) · Tz(d). The last row is the fixed step from the wrist to the flange (0.126 m).

Result: where is the tool?


    
The last column is the position of the flange in the base frame {0}, the third column the direction the tool points to. Both tables give exactly this same matrix.


    
Redundancy: one pose, infinitely many postures

    
The degree of redundancy is r = n − m = 7 − 6 = 1: when the tool pose is fixed, one parameter is still free. For this arm it is the elbow angle ψ, the rotation of the elbow around the line from the shoulder to the wrist (the “swivel” of your own elbow when you keep your hand on a table). Press the button under the 3D view: the controller holds the 6 coordinates of the tool and turns ψ; the line under the diagram shows that the tool does not move by more than a few hundredths of a millimetre.

    
How the button works: it turns q3 a little, then solves the inverse model of the six other joints so that the tool comes back exactly where it was (a damped least squares on the Jacobian, the method of the Python course). Real controllers do the same at the level of speeds, with the null space of the Jacobian: q̇ = J⁺ ẋ + (I − J⁺J) z.

Where is a 7-axis robot used?

      
Collaborative and sensitive robots (KUKA LBR iiwa and LBR Med, Franka Research 3, Kinova Gen3, ABB YuMi's arms), surgery and medical robots, research, assembly in tight spaces where the elbow must avoid obstacles, and space arms (the Canadarm2 of the International Space Station has 7 joints).

      
Strengths: it can reach around obstacles, avoid its joint limits and singularities, choose the posture that resists a force best, and move like a human arm; torque sensors in every joint (iiwa, Franka) make it safe next to people. Weaknesses: one more motor (cost, weight), the inverse model has no single answer and the controller must choose ψ, and payloads stay small (7 to 14 kg).

      
End effector (tool)

      
Grippers, screwdrivers, surgical instruments, cameras: the full 6 dof of the tool are available, plus the elbow for free.
