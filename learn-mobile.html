What is a wheeled mobile robot? Locomotion, kinematics and dynamics

10 min
You will learn

to say what makes a machine a robot, to name the five subsystems of a wheeled mobile robot, and to know what “kinematics” means for a robot that moves around.

1.1 What is a robot?

There is no precise definition, but by general agreement a robot is a machine with sensing, intelligence and mobility. To qualify, a machine has to be able to:

  1. sense and perceive: get information from its surroundings;
  2. carry out physical tasks: move itself (locomotion) or move objects (manipulation);
  3. be re-programmable: do different things when the program changes;
  4. work autonomously and interact with human beings.

1.2 Families of robots

Robot arms (manipulators) are fixed to the ground; a mobile manipulator is an arm carried by a mobile base. Mobile robots themselves come in several families: wheeled, legged, aerial, underwater and humanoid. This course is about the first family, the wheeled mobile robots (WMR): vacuum robots, warehouse AGV/AMR, planetary rovers, robot cars. They are the simplest mobile robots to build and to control, and they are the most common in factories and homes.

1.3 The five subsystems

A wheeled mobile robot combines hardware and software. It is a collection of subsystems:

SubsystemQuestion it answersExamples
LocomotionHow does the robot move through its environment?wheels, motors, gearboxes, chassis
SensingHow does it measure itself and its surroundings?encoders, gyroscope, lidar, camera
ControlHow does it generate physical actions?speed loops, position control
ReasoningHow does it map measurements to actions?localisation, path planning
CommunicationHow does it talk to other robots or to an operator?Wi-Fi, radio, ROS messages

In the navigation loop of a mobile robot, perception feeds localisation (“where am I?”) and cognition (“where do I go?”); cognition gives a path to motion control, which acts on the real world. This course is the locomotion and motion-control part of that loop.

1.4 Locomotion, dynamics and kinematics

  • Locomotion is the process of causing a robot to move. To produce motion, forces must be applied: motor output, payload, friction.
  • Dynamics studies motion with the forces that cause it: the relation between force and motion.
  • Kinematics studies the mathematics of motion without the forces: the geometric relations between the control parameters (wheel speeds, steering angles) and the behaviour of the robot.

Most mobile-robot controllers work at the kinematic level and ignore the dynamics, as long as the robot moves slowly enough. That is what we do here.

Why it is different from a robot arm

Kinematics of a mobile robot looks like that of an arm, but there is one big difference: an arm is fixed to the ground, so the position of its tool follows directly from the joint angles. A mobile robot moves without bound in its environment. There is no direct way to measure its position: it must be integrated over time from the wheel motions, and every small error is added to the next. This drift of the position estimate is the number 1 challenge of mobile robotics.

1.5 Three simple wheeled bases

Caterpillar (tracked) robotExact straight motion; robust to slipping.Turning is hard to model exactly.

Check yourself

  1. Name the five subsystems of a wheeled mobile robot.

    Locomotion, sensing, control, reasoning and communication.

  2. What is the difference between kinematics and dynamics?

    Kinematics relates positions and velocities geometrically, without forces. Dynamics also includes the forces and torques that produce the motion.

  3. Why can a mobile robot not simply read its position from a sensor like an arm does?

    Because it is not attached to the ground: its position has to be integrated from the motion of its wheels (odometry), and the errors accumulate.

Where is the robot? Pose, frames and the rotation matrix

11 min
You will learn

to describe the position and orientation of a robot on the floor with three numbers, and to convert a velocity between the global frame and the frame attached to the robot.

2.1 Three numbers: the pose

On a plane, the robot chassis has three dimensions: two for its position in the plane and one for its orientation around the vertical axis. The posture (or pose) is therefore:

ξI = ( x , y , θ )T    and its velocity    ξ̇I = ( ẋ , ẏ , θ̇ )T

The global frame {XI, YI} is fixed to the floor. The robot frame {XR, YR} is fixed to the chassis, with its origin at the reference point P, and XR pointing forward. The pose is the position of P in the global frame and the angle θ between the two frames.

2.2 From the global frame to the robot frame

The wheels act in the robot frame (“go forward, do not slide sideways”), but we want to know the motion in the global frame. The orthogonal rotation matrix converts one into the other:

R(θ) = cos θsin θ0− sin θcos θ0001     ξ̇R = R(θ) ξ̇I     ξ̇I = R(θ)−1 ξ̇R = R(θ)T ξ̇R

Because R is a rotation matrix, its inverse is simply its transpose. The same matrix will appear in every kinematic model of this course.

Worked example

The robot is rotated by θ = 90° (it points along YI). Then R(π/2) gives ξ̇R = ( ẏ , −ẋ , θ̇ ). A global velocity of ( 1 , 0 , 0 ) m/s along XI becomes ( 0 , −1 , 0 ) in the robot frame: the robot sees the world moving to its right, which is a sideways motion. This is the motion a standard wheel forbids.

With θ = 30° the same global velocity ( 1 , 0 , 0 ) gives ( cos 30° , −sin 30° , 0 ) = ( 0.87 , −0.5 , 0 ) in the robot frame.

2.3 Forward and inverse kinematics of a mobile robot

The goal is to write the speed of the robot as a function of the wheel speeds φ̇i, the steering angles βj and the geometry of the robot (the configuration coordinates):

  • forward kinematics: wheel speeds and steering → robot velocity ξ̇;
  • inverse kinematics: wanted velocity ξ̇ → wheel speeds and steering angles.

Be careful: we can write ξ̇ = f(wheel speeds), but not simply ξ = f(wheel angles). The pose depends on how the wheels were turned over time, not only on how far. We will see the exact reason in lesson 8 (nonholonomic systems).

Key terms

  • pose / posture
  • global (inertial) frame
  • robot frame
  • rotation matrix
  • forward / inverse kinematics
Check yourself

  1. How many numbers describe the pose of a robot on a flat floor?

    Three: x, y and the orientation θ.

  2. What is the inverse of R(θ)?

    Its transpose, which is also R(−θ).

  3. The robot has θ = 90° and moves at ẋ = 0, ẏ = 2 m/s in the global frame. What is its velocity in the robot frame?

    ξ̇_R = (ẏ, −ẋ, θ̇) = (2, 0, 0): it goes straight ahead at 2 m/s.

Wheels and their constraints: rolling and sliding

15 min
You will learn

to know the five wheel types, and to write the two constraints (rolling and no sliding) that each standard wheel imposes on the robot.

3.1 Five wheel types

WheelHow it worksEffect on the robot
Fixed standard wheelFixed to the chassis at an angle; rolls forward and backward; can pivot around its contact point.Constrains the motion: no sideways sliding.
Steered standard wheel (centred orientable)Same, plus a vertical steering axis through the contact point: angle β(t).Constrains the motion; steering changes the constraint over time.
Castor wheel (off-centred orientable)Steering axis offset by a distance d from the contact point, like a shopping-trolley wheel.No constraint on the chassis (the offset lets it follow any motion).
Swedish wheel (Mecanum, omni)A wheel with small rollers on its rim; the roller axis makes an angle γ with the wheel axis.No constraint: it can also move along the rollers.
Spherical wheel (ball)A ball that rolls in any direction.No constraint.

3.2 The assumptions

To get simple equations we model an idealised wheel:

  • the robot moves on a horizontal plane, and the wheel plane stays vertical;
  • the wheel touches the ground at one point and does not deform;
  • pure rolling: the velocity of the contact point is zero;
  • no slipping, skidding or sliding sideways, and no friction against rotation about the contact point;
  • the steering axes are perpendicular to the floor, and the wheels are connected by a rigid chassis.

Under these assumptions each standard wheel gives two constraints: rolling contact (the wheel must roll when the robot moves along its plane) and no lateral slip (the wheel must not slide across its plane).

3.3 The fixed standard wheel

The wheel is attached at a point A, given in polar coordinates by the distance l and the angle α from the reference point P. The angle of the wheel plane with respect to the chassis is β (constant for a fixed wheel). The wheel has radius r and turns at the speed φ̇.

Rolling:   [ sin(α+β)   −cos(α+β)   −l cos β ]   R(θ) ξ̇I − r φ̇ = 0

Sliding:   [ cos(α+β)   sin(α+β)   l sin β ]   R(θ) ξ̇I = 0

The first line says: the speed of the contact point along the wheel plane equals r φ̇ (the wheel really rolls). The second says: the speed across the wheel plane is zero (it does not slide). Each row projects the robot velocity on one direction of the wheel.

Worked example

A wheel is placed at α = 0, β = 0 (on the axis XR, its plane along YR), and the robot has θ = 0. The sliding constraint becomes [ 1 0 0 ] ( ẋ , ẏ , θ̇ )T = 0, that is ẋ = 0: the robot cannot move along X, which is across the wheel. It can still move along Y (rolling) and turn.

3.4 The other wheels

  • Steered wheel. Same two equations, but β = β(t) changes. The steering speed β̇ has no instantaneous effect on the constraints: only by integrating over time does a new steering angle change what the robot can do. Steering alone does not move the chassis.
  • Castor wheel. The rolling equation is the same, but the sliding equation gets two extra terms because of the offset d: [ cos(α+β)   sin(α+β)   d + l sin β ] R(θ) ξ̇I + d β̇ = 0. Any sideways motion can be absorbed by the right steering speed β̇, so the castor wheel imposes no constraint. Here the steering does move the chassis, because of the offset. A five-castor office chair rolls in any direction without sliding.
  • Swedish wheel. Add the roller angle γ: the zero-velocity direction is along the roller axis. Rolling: [ sin(α+β+γ)   −cos(α+β+γ)   −l cos(β+γ) ] R(θ) ξ̇I − r φ̇ cos γ = 0. The rollers turn freely, so the motion along the rollers is not constrained. For γ = 0 (the 90° Swedish wheel) the rollers are perpendicular to the wheel and the robot can slide sideways freely: this is the omni wheel. For γ = 90° the rollers are parallel to the wheel and the wheel behaves like a fixed standard wheel.
  • Spherical wheel. Its equations are those of the fixed standard wheel, but the angle β is a free variable instead of a constant: no constraint remains.
The key result

Castor, Swedish and spherical wheels put no kinematic constraint on the chassis: the robot velocity can take any value because of the internal degrees of freedom of these wheels. Only fixed and steered standard wheels constrain the chassis, so only they matter when we compute how a robot can move (lessons 7 and 8).

Key terms

  • rolling constraint
  • sliding constraint
  • fixed / steered standard wheel
  • castor wheel
  • Swedish (Mecanum) wheel
  • spherical wheel
Check yourself

  1. Which wheel types constrain the motion of the chassis?

    Only the fixed and the steered standard wheels.

  2. What do the two constraints of a standard wheel mean?

    Rolling: along its plane the wheel contact moves at r·φ̇. Sliding: across its plane the speed of the contact point is zero.

  3. Why does a castor wheel not constrain the robot, although it has a vertical axis like a steered wheel?

    Because its axis is offset by d from the contact point: a sideways motion is absorbed by a rotation of the steering axis (the term d·β̇).

Differential drive: the simplest mobile robot

16 min
You will learn

to compute how a two-wheel differential robot moves from its wheel speeds, and the opposite: which wheel speeds give the motion you want.

4.1 The robot

A differential-drive robot has two driven fixed wheels on the same axle, a distance L apart, each with its own motor, and usually a castor wheel or a ball for balance. It is the simplest drive mechanism and the one of most vacuum robots and educational robots. The control input is not the speed of each wheel but the motion of the robot itself:

U = ( v , ω )T    v = linear speed of the robot (m/s)    ω = angular speed (rad/s)

4.2 Wheel speeds ↔ robot motion

A wheel of radius r turning at φ̇ has a ground speed V = r φ̇. The left and right wheels turn about the same point at the same angular rate ω, so:

VR = r φ̇R    VL = r φ̇L      v = ( VR + VL ) / 2      ω = ( VR − VL ) / L

Inverse:   VR = v + ω L / 2     VL = v − ω L / 2

The speed of the robot is the average of the two wheel speeds, and its rotation is their difference divided by the distance between the wheels. If you drive the right wheel faster, the robot turns left (counterclockwise).

4.3 The turning circle and the ICC

At every instant both wheels follow circles around one point, the instantaneous centre of curvature (ICC, also called ICR). It lies on the axle line, at the distance R from the middle of the axle:

R = ( L / 2 ) · ( VR + VL ) / ( VR − VL ) = v / ω     ICC = ( x − R sin θ ,   y + R cos θ )

4.4 The posture model

In the global frame, the robot always moves along its heading, and turns at ω:

ẋẏθ̇ = cos θ0sin θ001 vω

In the robot frame, directly from the wheel angular speeds, the model is (with Vx along the robot and Vy across it):

VxVyθ̇ = r/2r/200−r/Lr/L φ̇Lφ̇R

The middle row is zero: the robot cannot move sideways. This is the sliding constraint of lesson 3, and it is also the origin of its difficulty: with 2 inputs v and ω the robot must reach 3 pose numbers.

4.5 The same result from the wheel constraints

Put the two wheels in the equations of lesson 3 (right wheel at α = −90°, β = 180°; left wheel at α = +90°, β = 0; l = L/2). The two rolling constraints and the (identical) sliding constraint stack into:

10L/210−L/2010 R(θ) ξ̇I = r φ̇Rr φ̇L0

Inverting this 3×3 matrix gives exactly the model above. Nothing was guessed: the kinematics of the robot comes from the constraints of its wheels. We will use this method again for the omnidirectional robot.

Worked example

Wheels of radius r = 0.05 m, distance L = 0.30 m. The motors run at φ̇R = 10 rad/s and φ̇L = 8 rad/s. Then VR = 0.50 m/s and VL = 0.40 m/s, so v = 0.45 m/s, ω = 0.10 / 0.30 = 0.33 rad/s, and R = v / ω = 1.35 m. A difference of only 2 rad/s between the motors already makes a circle of 2.7 m diameter. The robot turns left.

4.6 Odometry: computing the pose from the wheels

With encoders, each wheel gives the distance ΔsR and ΔsL it has rolled in one time step. Then:

Δs = ( ΔsR + ΔsL ) / 2    Δθ = ( ΔsR − ΔsL ) / L    x ← x + Δs cos( θ + Δθ/2 )   y ← y + Δs sin( θ + Δθ/2 )   θ ← θ + Δθ

Pitfall: small errors, big drift

The trajectory is very sensitive to the relative speed of the two wheels, not only to their average. A wheel diameter wrong by 1 % makes the robot curve instead of going straight, and odometry accumulates this error without limit. This is why real robots add a gyroscope, a lidar or a camera to correct the pose (see the “Perception” course).

  2. How do you make a differential robot spin on the spot?

Tricycle, bicycle and car: steering and the Ackermann rule

18 min
You will learn

to compute the wheel speeds and steering angles of a bicycle and of a car from the speed and the steering angle, and to explain why the two front wheels of a car do not turn by the same angle.

5.1 The tricycle

A tricycle has two fixed rear wheels and one front wheel that steers and drives, at the distance d in front of the rear axle. The inputs are the steering angle α(t) and the speed vs of the front wheel. All the wheel axes must meet at one point, so the ICC lies on the rear axle line, and the robot turns around it on a circle of radius:

R = d / tan α     v = vs cos α     ω = ( vs / d ) sin α

ẋẏθ̇ = cos θ0sin θ001 vs cos α(vs / d) sin α

The tricycle has a limited radius of curvature (it cannot spin on the spot) and cannot steer by ±90°. But it is simple and stable at speed.

5.2 Exercise: the bicycle

Question. A bicycle has a rear wheel (radius rF) and a front steering wheel (radius rC), a distance e apart. Knowing the speed v of the front wheel (along its direction) and the steering angle δ, find the angular velocities φ̇F of the rear wheel and φ̇C of the front wheel.

Solution. The front wheel rolls without slipping: its contact point moves at v along the wheel, so φ̇C = v / rC. The two wheels are on a rigid frame, so their velocity components along the line that joins them are equal. The rear wheel moves along the frame at vr, the front wheel has the component v cos δ along it. Hence vr = v cos δ, and φ̇F = v cos δ / rF.

φ̇C = v / rC     φ̇F = v cos δ / rF     ( ω = v sin δ / e ,   R = e / tan δ )

Worked example

rF = rC = 0.35 m, e = 1.1 m, v = 3 m/s, δ = 20°. Then φ̇C = 3 / 0.35 = 8.57 rad/s and φ̇F = 3 cos 20° / 0.35 = 8.05 rad/s: the rear wheel turns slower than the front one in a turn. The bicycle turns at ω = 3 sin 20° / 1.1 = 0.93 rad/s, around a point at R = 1.1 / tan 20° = 3.02 m from the rear wheel. If δ = 0 the two speeds are equal.

5.3 Exercise: the car

Question. Now a car with two rear wheels F1, F2 (track L) and two steering front wheels C1, C2 (wheelbase e). Knowing v and the steering angle δ of the “virtual” central wheel, find the four wheel speeds φ̇F1, φ̇F2, φ̇C1, φ̇C2 and the two steering angles δC1, δC2.

Solution. Replace the car by its “bicycle” in the middle: it gives the ICC on the rear axle line, at R = e / tan δ from the middle of the rear axle, and the rotation ω = v sin δ / e. Every wheel is then a point that turns around the ICC at the same ω: its speed is ω times its distance to the ICC, and its plane is perpendicular to the line to the ICC. For a left turn, wheel 1 (left) is on the inside:

φ̇F1 = ω ( R − L/2 ) / rF1    φ̇F2 = ω ( R + L/2 ) / rF2

tan δC1 = e / ( R − L/2 )    tan δC2 = e / ( R + L/2 )    φ̇Ci = ω √( e² + ( R ∓ L/2 )² ) / rCi

Worked example

e = 2.5 m, L = 1.4 m, all radii 0.30 m, v = 5 m/s, δ = 15°. Then R = 2.5 / tan 15° = 9.33 m and ω = 5 sin 15° / 2.5 = 0.518 rad/s. Steering angles: δC1 = 16.2° (inside), δC2 = 14.0° (outside). Wheel speeds: φ̇F1 = 14.9, φ̇F2 = 17.3, φ̇C1 = 15.5, φ̇C2 = 17.8 rad/s. The outside wheels turn faster because they travel on a bigger circle: this is what the differential gear of a car allows.

5.4 The Ackermann rule

In a car the inside front wheel is turned slightly more than the outside one. This is not a detail: the axes of the four wheels must meet at the single ICC, otherwise the tyres drag sideways, wear and slip. With the track d (= L) and the wheelbase l (= e), this gives the Ackermann equation, where the angles are measured from the straight-ahead direction:

cot δoutside − cot δinside = d / l     cot δ = ( cot δinside + cot δoutside ) / 2

Check with the example: cot 14.0° − cot 16.2° = 4.01 − 3.45 = 0.56 = 1.4 / 2.5. ✓ The mean cotangent is that of the central “virtual” wheel δ = 15°. Ackermann steering gives a fairly accurate dead-reckoning solution and supports traction and ground clearance, which is why it is the usual choice for outdoor autonomous vehicles.

What a car cannot do

A car has only 2 control inputs (speed and steering) for 3 pose numbers, and its steering angle is limited, so it needs a series of manoeuvres to park sideways. Lesson 7 will count exactly how much freedom it has.

  1. A bicycle with two wheels of radius 0.30 m goes at v = 4 m/s with δ = 30°. Find φ̇_C and φ̇_F.

    φ̇_C = 4 / 0.30 = 13.3 rad/s. φ̇_F = 4 cos 30° / 0.30 = 11.5 rad/s.

  2. A tricycle has d = 0.6 m and the steering angle is 45°. What is its turning radius?

Omnidirectional robots and synchro drive

15 min
You will learn

to compute the motion of a three-wheel omnidirectional robot from its wheel speeds (and the reverse), and to explain how a synchro drive works.

6.1 Why omnidirectional?

A robot with fixed standard wheels cannot move sideways. A robot with Swedish (omni) wheels can: each wheel drives along its own plane and rolls freely across it on the small rollers. With three such wheels, any combination of forward speed, sideways speed and rotation can be commanded at once: the robot is holonomic with 3 independent velocities. Soccer robots, warehouse platforms and stage robots use this. The price is a complex structure, weak frames (small rollers), slipping on dust, and bumpy motion on rough floors.

6.2 Three Swedish 90° wheels

Place three identical Swedish 90° wheels at the distance l from the centre P, at the angles α1 = 60°, α2 = 180°, α3 = −60°, with β = 0 (wheels tangent to the body) and γ = 0. There is no sliding constraint, so only the three rolling constraints remain. Each row of the matrix J1 is the rolling row of lesson 3:

J1 = √3 / 2−1/2−l01−l−√3 / 2−1/2−l     J1 R(θ) ξ̇I = r ( φ̇1 , φ̇2 , φ̇3 )T

This is the inverse kinematics directly: the wheel speeds come from the velocity wanted. For the forward kinematics, invert the 3×3 matrix (it is always invertible):

ξ̇I = R(θ)−1 1/√30−1/√3−1/32/3−1/3−1/(3l)−1/(3l)−1/(3l) r φ̇1φ̇2φ̇3

Worked example

l = 1, r = 1, wheel speeds φ̇ = ( 4 , 1 , 2 ) rad/s and θ = 0. Then ẋ = ( 4 − 2 ) / √3 = 1.15, ẏ = ( −4 + 2 − 2 ) / 3 = −1.33, θ̇ = −( 4 + 1 + 2 ) / 3 = −2.33 rad/s. The robot moves and turns at the same time, with all the three speeds computed from the three wheels. If all three wheels run at the same speed, ẋ = ẏ = 0 and the robot spins on the spot.

This is the general method of the course: write the rolling and sliding constraint of every wheel, stack them, and solve. Four Mecanum wheels in an “X” layout work the same way, with the wheel rollers at ±45°: see the wheels page to drive one.

6.3 Synchro drive

In a synchro drive, each of three wheels (at the corners of an equilateral triangle, often under a cylindrical body) is both driven and steered, but a system of belts links them so that all the wheels point in the same direction and turn at the same rate. One motor drives all the wheels, one motor steers them all. So the robot has exactly two independent inputs: the speed v and the direction of travel.

  • All wheels stay parallel, so the ICC is always at infinity: the robot translates, it never moves on a curve by itself. Turning the wheels changes the direction of the ICC.
  • v = 0 with the wheels turning: the wheels rotate in place. ω = 0 with v ≠ 0: the robot goes straight in the direction its wheels point.
  • The body of the robot does not turn when the wheels are steered. To keep sensors facing the travel direction, a third motor and a turret are needed. This is a classic design for research robots with a cylindrical body.

Compared with a differential robot, a synchro drive can change its direction of travel without turning its body, and the belts keep all the wheels locked together. But it is mechanically complex, and lesson 7 will show that it has only 2 degrees of maneuverability: it cannot rotate its body without the turret.

Check yourself

  1. A three-wheel omni robot has the three wheels turning at the same speed. How does it move?

    It spins on the spot: ẋ = ẏ = 0 and θ̇ = −r(φ̇₁+φ̇₂+φ̇₃)/(3l).

  2. Why does a three-wheel omni robot have no sliding constraint?

  3. Where is the ICC of a synchro drive?

    At infinity, in the direction perpendicular to the wheels: all wheel axes are parallel.

7

Maneuverability: how freely can a robot move?

17 min
You will learn

to stack the constraints of all the wheels, to find the instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR) from them, and to compute the degrees of mobility, steerability and maneuverability of any wheel layout.

7.1 Stacking the constraints

Take a robot with Nf fixed and Ns steered standard wheels (the only ones that constrain the chassis, lesson 3). Put their rolling and sliding rows one under the other:

Rolling:   J1(βs) R(θ) ξ̇I − J2 φ̇ = 0     Sliding:   C1(βs) R(θ) ξ̇I = 0

J1 and C1 have one row per standard wheel (Nf + Ns rows) and 3 columns; J2 = diag(r1 … rN) holds the wheel radii; φ̇ is the vector of the wheel speeds. The sliding equation is the important one for what the robot can do: the velocity R(θ) ξ̇I must lie in the null space of C1, the set of the vectors n such that C1 n = 0.

7.2 The ICR: a picture of the null space

Geometrically, each standard wheel forbids the motion along its axle. At every instant the wheel must move along a circle whose centre is on the zero-motion line (the line along its axle, perpendicular to its plane). The point where all the zero-motion lines meet is the instantaneous centre of rotation (ICR): the robot turns around it.

  • Bicycle: the rear wheel and the steered front wheel each give one line; they meet at one point.
  • Differential drive: both wheels share the same axle line, so they give the same line: the ICR can be anywhere along it.
  • Car: four wheels but only one ICR; the second steered wheel adds no new constraint (it must simply point to the same ICR, Ackermann rule).
Mobility depends on independent constraints, not on the number of wheels

A bicycle and a differential robot both have two non-omnidirectional wheels, but the bicycle has two independent constraints and the differential robot only one, because the second wheel has the same zero-motion line as the first. The number of independent constraints is the rank of C1(βs): the higher the rank, the more constrained the robot.

7.3 The three degrees

Three wheels are enough for a robot to stand stable on its own; extra wheels must be synchronised (or suspended). Two robots with the same δM are not equivalent. Worked example: the rank of a differential robot and of a locked bicycle

Differential drive: both wheels have the row [ cos α1   sin α1   0 ] and its negative: the two rows are proportional, so rank C1 = 1 and δm = 3 − 1 = 2. Locked bicycle (steering fixed straight): the rows [ 0   1   l1 ] and [ 0   −1   l1 ] are independent, so rank = 2 and δm = 1: the robot can only move along a curve (or a line).

8

Holonomic or not? Degrees of freedom, paths and trajectories

14 min
You will learn

to explain what “nonholonomic” means, why a car cannot go sideways but can still park, and what the difference is between a path and a trajectory.

8.1 DOF, DDOF and maneuverability

Maneuverability δM counts the degrees of freedom we can control. We also ask: what poses can the robot reach in its workspace, and how?

  • DOF (degrees of freedom of the workspace): the poses the robot can reach. For a mobile robot on a floor we want DOF = 3 ( x, y, θ ).
  • DDOF (differential degrees of freedom): the velocities the robot can produce independently at one instant, which is the degree of mobility δm. It tells which paths are possible.

DDOF ≤ δM ≤ DOF

A bicycle has DOF = 3 (it can end at any pose) but DDOF = 1 (at each instant it can only drive forward or backward along its heading). An omnidirectional robot has DOF = 3 and DDOF = 3. Both reach every pose, but the omnidirectional robot reaches it directly, and the bicycle needs a sequence of manoeuvres like parallel parking.

8.2 Holonomic and nonholonomic constraints

A holonomic kinematic constraint can be written as a function of the position variables only (x, y, θ, wheel angles…). A nonholonomic constraint needs derivatives (velocities) and cannot be integrated to remove them. Such systems are also called nonintegrable. A robot is holonomic if and only if DDOF = DOF.

  • The sliding constraint of a fixed or steered standard wheel, ẋ sin θ − ẏ cos θ = 0 for a differential robot, uses the velocity: it is nonholonomic.
  • Differential drive, tricycle, car, two-steer: nonholonomic (DDOF < DOF = 3).
  • Omnidirectional robot: no constraint at all, holonomic with DDOF = 3.
  • Locked bicycle (steering blocked): all constraints can be integrated into constraints on the pose, it can only move on a curve: holonomic, but of no interest (DOF = 1).

8.3 Proof that a mobile robot is nonholonomic

Take a robot that moves along a trajectory s(t). In a short time the distance travelled is ds = dx cos θ + dy sin θ. This could be integrated into a function s(x, y, θ) of the pose alone only if the second derivatives of s do not depend on the order of derivation:

∂²s / ∂x∂θ = ∂²s / ∂θ∂x    etc.

Here ∂s/∂x = cos θ, ∂s/∂y = sin θ and ∂s/∂θ = 0. Then ∂/∂θ(∂s/∂x) = −sin θ, but ∂/∂x(∂s/∂θ) = 0: they are different, so no such function exists. In words: the distance travelled by each wheel is not enough to know where the robot is. Two robots can roll the same distances with their wheels, but ending in different places, because they did it in a different order. We must also know how the motion was done, as a function of time. This is why odometry integrates the speeds step by step, and why the pose is not a function of the wheel angles.

8.4 Path versus trajectory

A path is a curve in pose space (x, y, θ): 3 dimensions. A trajectory adds time: (x, y, θ, t), 4 dimensions. The two kinds of robots with δM = 3 differ here:

  • The omnidirectional robot controls all 3 velocities directly (δm = 3): it can follow any trajectory, at any speed along it. But it is not stable at high speed (centripetal forces on wheels that do not resist sideways), and it needs ground clearance and suspension for its special wheels.
  • The two-steer robot (δm = 1, δs = 2) can choose any ICR, so it can follow any path. But changing the steering takes time, so arbitrary trajectories are not possible.
  • A car-like robot with nonholonomic constraints is more stable: the sideways centripetal force in a turn is passively counteracted by the sliding constraints of its wheels. This is one reason why cars and fast outdoor robots are nonholonomic on purpose.

  2. Why does a robot with a pose function s(x, y, θ) not exist for the distance travelled?

9

Motion control: driving a robot to a goal pose

18 min
You will learn

to compare open-loop and feedback control for a mobile robot, to write the polar-coordinate control law of a differential robot, and to choose gains that make it converge.

9.1 The goal of kinematic control

A kinematic controller makes the robot follow a trajectory described by position and/or velocity profiles as functions of time. It is not straightforward, because mobile robots are nonholonomic: you cannot just move to the right. Still, several good solutions exist. Most of them ignore the dynamics of the system and give the speeds that the wheels must have (lesson 4).

9.2 Open-loop control

Divide the path into segments of clearly defined shapes, straight lines and circle arcs, and pre-compute the speeds of each. The disadvantages:

  • it is not easy to pre-compute a feasible trajectory;
  • it must respect the limits of the robot velocities and accelerations;
  • it does not correct errors, or adapt if the environment changes;
  • the resulting trajectories are usually not smooth (the curvature jumps between a line and a circle).

9.3 Feedback control: the problem

With feedback, the controller uses the error e between the current pose and the goal: we look for a matrix K (2×3, its elements may depend on time and on the error), such that the commands

( v , ω )T = K · e = K · ( x , y , θ )T    drive the error to zero:   limt→∞ e(t) = 0

Because the robot is nonholonomic, a constant matrix K cannot do it: the gains must depend on the error (kij = k(t, e)). A convenient way to get such gains is to change the coordinates.

9.4 Polar coordinates of the goal

Put the origin of a frame at the goal and describe the robot by three polar quantities, where Δx, Δy is the vector from the robot to the goal:

ρ = √( Δx² + Δy² )    α = −θ + atan2( Δy , Δx )    β = −θ − α    (goal angle θg = 0)

  • ρ: the distance to the goal;
  • α: the angle between the robot heading and the direction of the goal (“am I pointing at it?”);
  • β: the angle between the line of sight and the goal orientation (“am I arriving the right way?”).

With the kinematics of the differential robot (ẋ = v cos θ, ẏ = v sin θ, θ̇ = ω), the system becomes, for α in (−90°, 90°]:

ρ̇ = −v cos α     α̇ = ( v sin α ) / ρ − ω     β̇ = −( v sin α ) / ρ

For α outside this range the robot has the goal behind it: the same equations hold with the robot's “front” redefined as its back, and v changes sign: the robot drives backwards and arrives with its back to the goal direction (θ ≈ 180° instead of 0). Note also that the transformation is not defined at ρ = 0 (the goal itself), where the angles have no meaning.

9.5 The control law

v = kρ ρ     ω = kα α + kβ β

It is a proportional law: go faster when far from the goal, turn toward the goal with kα, and turn to arrive in the right orientation with kβ. Closed loop, the system is:

ρ̇ = −kρ ρ cos α     α̇ = kρ sin α − kα α − kβ β     β̇ = −kρ sin α

Stability: how to choose the gains

For small α, sin α ≈ α and cos α ≈ 1, and the system is linear. Its characteristic polynomial is ( λ + kρ ) ( λ² + λ ( kα − kρ ) − kρ kβ ). All its roots have a negative real part, so the robot converges to ( ρ, α, β ) = ( 0, 0, 0 ), if:

kρ > 0     kβ < 0     kα − kρ > 0

A textbook choice is kρ = 3, kα = 8, kβ = −1.5. Note that ρ then shrinks faster than the angles converge, so the final heading is a little off when you stop at a distance tolerance: a smaller kρ (about 1 to 1.5) and a larger |kβ| give a more precise heading, but a slower approach. Another nice property: the speed v keeps a constant sign, so the robot never reverses during its approach: it parks in the most natural way.

Finally the commands v and ω are converted into wheel speeds with VR = v + ω L/2 and VL = v − ω L/2 (lesson 4), and the motors' own speed loops (lesson “Dynamics and control”) follow them. In practice, add saturations on v and on the wheel speeds, and stop when ρ is below a tolerance, since the law has no meaning at the goal itself.

Check yourself

  1. Why is open-loop control with lines and circles weak?

  2. With kρ = 2, kα = 5, kβ = −1, is the controller stable?

    Yes: kρ > 0, kβ < 0 and kα − kρ = 3 > 0.

  3. What do α and β mean?

    α is the angle between the robot heading and the direction to the goal; β is the angle between the line of sight and the goal orientation.
