1

Angles and trigonometry

10 min
You will learn

to measure an angle in degrees and radians, to use sine and cosine to find a point, and atan2 to find an angle.

1.1 Degrees and radians

A robot joint turns by an angle. We know degrees: a full turn is 360°. Computers and formulas use radians: a full turn is 2π ≈ 6.28 rad. So:

radians = degrees × π / 180 degrees = radians × 180 / π

Degrees0°30°45°90°180°360°
Radians0π/6 ≈ 0.52π/4 ≈ 0.79π/2 ≈ 1.57π ≈ 3.142π ≈ 6.28
Why radians?

With radians, the length of an arc is simply radius × angle. A wheel of radius 0.1 m that turns by 2 rad rolls 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 m. No factor to remember.

1.2 Sine and cosine: where is the end of the arm?

Take an arm of length L, fixed at the origin, tilted by an angle θ from the x axis. Its end is at:

x = L cos θ y = L sin θ

The cosine gives the part of the arm along x, the sine the part along y. Values to know by heart:

θ0°30°45°60°90°180°
cos θ10.8660.7070.50−1
sin θ00.50.7070.86610
Worked example

An arm of 0.8 m at 30°: x = 0.8 × 0.866 = 0.693 m, y = 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4 m. Check with Pythagoras: √(0.693² + 0.4²) = 0.8 m, the length of the arm.

1.3 Two formulas you will meet everywhere

  • Pythagoras: cos² θ + sin² θ = 1. The distance from the origin to the point (x, y) is √(x² + y²).
  • Sum of angles: cos(a + b) = cos a cos b − sin a sin b, and sin(a + b) = sin a cos b + cos a sin b. This is why a 2-axis arm has terms in cos(θ1 + θ2).

1.4 The other way: from a point to an angle (atan2)

You know the point (x, y) and want the angle. Use θ = atan2(y, x), found in every programming language. Note the order: y first.

Never use arctan(y / x) alone

The points (1, 1) and (−1, −1) have the same ratio y/x = 1, so arctan gives 45° for both. But the second one is at −135°. atan2 looks at the signs of x and y and gives the right angle over the full turn, even when x = 0.

Key terms

  • radian
  • sine / cosine
  • Pythagoras
  • atan2
The essentials
  • Radians = degrees × π / 180. A full turn is 2π.
  • The end of an arm of length L at angle θ is at x = L cos θ, y = L sin θ.
  • To find an angle from a point, use atan2(y, x), never arctan(y/x) alone.

Vectors

11 min
You will learn

what a vector is, how to add vectors, measure their length, and use the dot and cross products.

2.1 An arrow with numbers

A vector is an arrow: it has a direction and a length. We write it as a column of numbers, its components: 31 means “3 to the right, 1 up”. In space there is a third number for the height: xyz.

In robotics vectors describe positions (where the tool is), speeds (how it moves), forces (how it pushes) and axes (around what a joint turns).

2.2 Adding vectors: one arrow after the other

To chain two moves, add the components one by one: 31 + 12 = 43.

This is a robot arm

Look at the picture again: the blue arrow is link 1, the orange arrow is link 2, and the dashed arrow goes from the base to the tool. The position of the tool is the sum of the arrows of the links. All of forward kinematics is in this sentence.

2.3 Length, scaling, unit vector

  • Length (norm): ‖v‖ = √(x² + y² + z²). For (4, 3): √(16 + 9) = 5.
  • Multiply by a number: every component is multiplied. 2 × (4, 3) = (8, 6): same direction, twice as long.
  • Unit vector: a vector of length 1, used to give a direction only. Divide a vector by its length: (4, 3) / 5 = (0.8, 0.6).

2.4 The dot product: how much two arrows agree

a · b = axbx + ayby + azbz = ‖a‖ ‖b‖ cos φ

The result is a number. φ is the angle between the two arrows. Three cases to remember:

The arrows are…Dot productExample
in the same directionpositive, maximum(1, 0) · (2, 0) = 2
perpendicularzero(1, 0) · (0, 3) = 0
oppositenegative(1, 0) · (−2, 0) = −2

Use: the dot product with a unit vector gives the part of a vector along that direction (the speed of a robot along its forward axis, the force along a joint axis).

2.5 The cross product: a vector perpendicular to two others

In 3D, a × b is a new vector, perpendicular to both a and b. Its direction is given by the right hand: index along a, middle finger along b, the thumb shows a × b.

x × y = z y × z = x z × x = y

  • It builds the third axis of a frame from the two others (lessons 5 and 6).
  • It gives the speed of a point that turns: v = ω × r, and the torque of a force: τ = r × F.

Key terms

  • vector
  • component
  • norm
  • unit vector
  • dot product
  • cross product
The essentials
  • A vector is an arrow written as a column of numbers. Add vectors component by component.
  • Length: √(x² + y² + z²). A unit vector has length 1.
  • Dot product = 0 means perpendicular. The cross product gives a perpendicular vector.

Matrices

12 min
You will learn

to multiply a matrix by a vector and by another matrix, and what the identity, the transpose, the inverse and the determinant are.

3.1 A table that transforms a vector

A matrix is a table of numbers. Its job: take a vector and give back another vector (turned, stretched, moved). The rule is “row times column”: multiply the numbers of a row by the numbers of the column, one by one, and add.

abcd xy = ax + bycx + dy

Worked example

1234 56 = 1×5 + 2×63×5 + 4×6 = 1739

What the columns mean

The first column of a matrix is where the vector 10 (the x axis) goes; the second column is where 01 (the y axis) goes. To build a matrix that does what you want, ask: “where do the axes go?” and write the answers as columns. Lesson 4 uses this to build the rotation matrix.

3.2 Matrix times matrix: two transformations in a row

The product A B is the matrix that does B first, then A. Same rule: the element in row i, column j of the result is row i of A “times” column j of B.

1234 0110 = 1×0 + 2×11×1 + 2×03×0 + 4×13×1 + 4×0 = 2143

The order matters

In general A B ≠ B A. With the same two matrices in the other order: 0110 1234 = 3412. It is normal: “turn then move forward” does not lead to the same place as “move forward then turn”.

Worked example: determinant and inverse

A = 2111: det A = 2×1 − 1×1 = 1, so A−1 = 1−1−12. Check: A−1 A = 1×2 − 1×11×1 − 1×1−1×2 + 2×1−1×1 + 2×1 = 1001.

  2. Yes

Check yourself

  1. What do the columns of a matrix tell you?

    Where the axes go: column 1 is the image of (1, 0), column 2 the image of (0, 1).

  2. In the product A B applied to a vector, which matrix acts first?

    B, the one closest to the vector. Then A.

  3. A matrix has a determinant of 0. Can you invert it?

    No. It flattens space onto a line or a point, and this cannot be undone.

Rotation matrices

12 min
You will learn

to build the matrix that turns a vector by an angle, its properties, and the three rotations of 3D space.

4.1 Build it from its columns

We want the matrix that turns every vector by an angle θ (counter-clockwise). Use the idea of lesson 3: where do the axes go?

  • The x axis 10 turns to cos θsin θ (lesson 1). This is the first column.
  • The y axis 01 turns to −sin θcos θ. This is the second column.
The rotation matrix in the plane

R(θ) = cos θ−sin θsin θcos θ

Worked example

Turn the vector 21 by 30° (cos = 0.866, sin = 0.5): 0.866−0.50.50.866 21 = 0.866×2 − 0.5×10.5×2 + 0.866×1 = 1.2321.866. The length did not change: √(2² + 1²) = √(1.232² + 1.866²) = 2.236.

4.2 Four properties to remember

PropertyWhyWhat it gives you
The columns have length 1 and are perpendicular.They are the axes of a frame.A quick check of any rotation matrix.
det R = 1cos² θ + sin² θ = 1: a rotation does not change sizes.A rotation can always be undone.
R−1 = RT = R(−θ)To undo a rotation, turn back by the same angle.The inverse costs nothing: swap rows and columns.
R(a) R(b) = R(a + b)Turning by b then by a is turning by a + b.In a planar arm the angles add: θ1 + θ2.

4.3 Rotations in space

In 3D a rotation turns around an axis. The positive direction is given by the right hand: thumb along the axis, the fingers show the positive rotation. The three basic rotations keep one axis fixed (its row and column are 0, 0, 1):

Rz(θ) = c−s0sc0001 Ry(θ) = c0s010−s0c Rx(θ) = 1000c−s0sc

with c = cos θ and s = sin θ. Rz is the plane rotation of 4.1 with a third axis that does not move: it is the rotation of a revolute joint around its own z axis, the one you will meet in every DH table.

In 3D the order of rotations matters

Take the x axis (1, 0, 0). Turn it by 90° around x (nothing happens), then by 90° around z: it becomes (0, 1, 0). In the other order, 90° around z gives (0, 1, 0), then 90° around x gives (0, 0, 1). Two different results. In the plane the order did not matter; in space it does. Try it with a book in your hands.

Key terms

  • rotation matrix
  • right-hand rule
  • orthogonal matrix
The essentials
  • R(θ) has the columns (cos θ, sin θ) and (−sin θ, cos θ): where the axes go.
  • A rotation keeps lengths; its inverse is its transpose.
  • In the plane the angles add; in 3D the order of rotations matters.

Worked exercises

Solution

The first column of R(90°) is (cos 90°, sin 90°) = (0, 1): y = 1.

Level 2 · Intermediate2

Solution

Level 3 · Professional3

A planar arm turns by 30° at joint 1 and by −50° at joint 2. Orientation of the tool (degrees)?

Check yourself

  1. What is the first column of R(θ)?

    (cos θ, sin θ): where the x axis goes.

  2. How do you invert a rotation matrix?

    Take its transpose: R⁻¹ = Rᵀ.

  3. Does the order of two rotations matter?

    Not in the plane (the angles add). In 3D yes, when the axes differ.

5

Frames and transformations

14 min
You will learn

to go from one frame to another with a rotation and a translation, and to pack both in one homogeneous matrix that you can chain.

5.1 The same point, seen from two frames

A frame is an origin and axes: a place from which you measure. A robot has many: one on the floor, one on each link, one on the tool, one on the camera. The same point has different coordinates in each frame.

Frame B sits at t = (3, 1) in frame A, and is turned by 90°. The point P is at (2, 1) for B. Where is it for A? Two steps: turn the coordinates with the rotation of B, then add the position of B.

Change of frame

pA = R pB + t

R = the orientation of B seen from A (its columns are the axes of B, written in A). t = the origin of B, written in A.

Worked example

R(90°) = 0−110. pA = 0−110 21 + 31 = −12 + 31 = 23. Check on the picture: from B, go 2 along xB (up) and 1 along yB (left).

5.2 One matrix for both: the homogeneous matrix

“Multiply, then add” is not practical to chain ten frames. The trick: add a 1 under every point, and put R and t in one bigger matrix. One product now does the rotation and the translation:

pA1 = Rt01 pB1 ATB = Rt01

In the plane it is a 3×3 matrix; in space a 4×4 matrix (R is 3×3, t has 3 numbers, the last row is always 0 0 0 1). With our example: 0−13101001 211 = 0×2 − 1×1 + 3×11×2 + 0×1 + 1×11 = 231.

How to read a homogeneous matrix
  • The last column is the position of the frame: where it is.
  • The other columns are its axes: how it is turned.
  • A point ends with 1 (the translation applies); a direction ends with 0 (it is only turned).

5.3 Chaining frames: this is forward kinematics

0T2 = 0T1 1T2

To go from frame 2 to frame 0, go through frame 1: multiply the matrices. Reading trick: the indices that touch “cancel” (the 1 below and the 1 above). For a robot with n joints: 0Tn = 0T1 1T2 … n−1Tn, and the last column of the result is the position of the tool.

Worked example: a 2-axis arm

L1 = 0.5 m, L2 = 0.4 m, θ1 = 30°, θ2 = 45°. Each matrix says “turn by θ, then move forward by L along the new x”: its last column is (L cos θ, L sin θ).

0T1 = 0.866−0.50.4330.50.8660.25001 1T2 = 0.707−0.7070.2830.7070.7070.283001 0T2 = 0.259−0.9660.5370.9660.2590.636001

The tool is at x = 0.537 m, y = 0.636 m. The same as the sum of arrows of lesson 2: 0.5 cos 30° + 0.4 cos 75° = 0.537 and 0.5 sin 30° + 0.4 sin 75° = 0.636. And the top-left block is a rotation of 75° = 30° + 45°: the orientation of the tool.

5.4 Going back: the inverse

(ATB)−1 = BTA = RT−RT t01

No heavy computation: transpose the rotation, and turn the translation back. It answers the opposite question: “where is A, seen from B?”

6

The Denavit–Hartenberg method (standard DH)

15 min
You will learn

where the standard DH convention puts the frames, its four parameters, its matrix, and how to write the DH table of a robot step by step.

6.1 The problem: too much freedom

Lesson 5 showed that a robot is a chain of frames, one per link, and that we multiply their matrices. But where exactly do we put each frame on its link? If everybody chooses freely, a frame needs 6 numbers (3 for the position, 3 for the orientation), and nobody gets the same equations.

In 1955 Jacques Denavit and Richard Hartenberg proposed a rule to place the frames. With this rule, going from one frame to the next needs only 4 numbers. A whole robot becomes a small table: one row per joint, four numbers per row.

6.2 Where the frames go

Number the links from 0 (the base) to n (the tool), and the joints from 1 to n: joint i connects link i−1 to link i. In standard DH, frame i is fixed to link i, at its far end, on the axis of the next joint:

Standard DH: placing frame i
  1. zi is along the axis of joint i+1 (so z0 is the axis of joint 1, z1 the axis of joint 2…).
  2. xi is along the common perpendicular: the shortest segment from the axis zi−1 to the axis zi. It must cross zi−1 at a right angle.
  3. The origin is where xi meets zi.
  4. yi completes the frame (right-hand rule: y = z × x). You never need to draw it.

6.3 The four parameters

To go from frame i−1 to frame i, do four simple moves in this order: two along the old axis zi−1, then two along the new axis xi.

OrderParameterMoveIn plain words
1θiturn around zi−1the joint angle: the variable of a revolute joint
2dislide along zi−1the offset along the joint axis: the variable of a prismatic joint
3aislide along xithe link length: the distance between the two joint axes
4αiturn around xithe twist: the angle between the two joint axes (0 if parallel, ±90° if perpendicular)
What moves, what is fixed
  • ai and αi never change: they are the shape of link i.
  • Revolute joint: θi is the variable, di is fixed. Prismatic joint: di is the variable, θi is fixed.

6.4 The matrix of one row

Each move is a homogeneous matrix (lesson 5). Multiply the four, in the order of the table:

i−1Ti = Rot(z, θi) · Trans(z, di) · Trans(x, ai) · Rot(x, αi)

i−1Ti = cθ−sθ cαsθ sαa cθsθcθ cα−cθ sαa sθ0sαcαd0001

with cθ = cos θi, sα = sin αi… You do not need to learn it by heart: know where it comes from, and let a program fill it.

6.5 Example 1: the 2-axis arm

The two joint axes point out of the page, so they are parallel: every αi = 0 and every di = 0. Frame 1 is at the end of link 1, frame 2 at the end of link 2 (the tool).

6.6 Example 2: a 3-axis arm in space

The arm of the “Build your first robot” workshop: joint 1 turns around the vertical; joints 2 and 3 (shoulder, elbow) turn around a horizontal axis. Shoulder height 0.40 m, links of 0.45 m and 0.38 m.

Check with numbers

θ1 = 25°, θ2 = 70°, θ3 = −80°. Multiplying the three matrices gives the tool at (0.479, 0.223, 0.757) m. By hand: r = 0.45 cos 70° + 0.38 cos(−10°) = 0.528; x = r cos 25° = 0.479; y = r sin 25° = 0.223; z = 0.40 + 0.45 sin 70° + 0.38 sin(−10°) = 0.757. The table and the drawing agree. Set these angles in the workshop: it shows the same position.

Three special cases
  • Parallel axes: there are many common perpendiculars. Choose the one that makes d = 0.
  • Axes that cross: a = 0, and x is perpendicular to both axes (two possible directions: choose one, it sets the sign of α).
  • Zero position: the arm drawn with all θ = 0 may not be the “home” pose of the real robot. A fixed offset is then added in the table, written θ + 90° for example.

7

The modified DH method (DHM)

14 min
You will learn

what changes in the modified convention, where its frames go, its four parameters, its matrix, and the DHM tables of the same two robots.

7.1 One idea changes: the frame sits on its own joint

In standard DH, frame i is at the far end of link i, on the axis of joint i+1. Many people find this confusing: the frame of link 2 is on joint 3. The modified convention (Khalil and Kleinfinger, 1986; also used in the book of J. Craig) moves each frame to the start of its link:

The rule of DHM

Frame j is fixed to link j and sits on the axis of joint j: the joint that moves this link. Joint 1 → frame 1, joint 2 → frame 2. Same number everywhere.

Modified DH: placing frame j
  1. zj is along the axis of joint j.
  2. xj is along the common perpendicular from zj to the next axis zj+1.
  3. The origin is where xj meets zj.
  4. yj completes the frame.

7.2 The four parameters, in a new order

To go from frame j−1 to frame j: first the shape of the previous link (two moves along xj−1), then the motion of joint j (two moves along zj).

OrderParameterMoveIn plain words
1αj−1turn around xj−1the twist between the axes zj−1 and zj
2aj−1slide along xj−1the distance between these two axes
3θjturn around zjthe joint angle: the variable of a revolute joint
4djslide along zjthe offset along the joint axis: the variable of a prismatic joint

The meaning of the four numbers is the same as in standard DH: a twist, a distance, a joint angle, an offset. What changes is the order and the indices: the twist and the distance of row j belong to the link before joint j.

j−1Tj = Rot(x, αj−1) · Trans(x, aj−1) · Rot(z, θj) · Trans(z, dj)

Two notations for the same DHM

Books do not use the same letters. This lesson and the robot pages write the row (αj−1, aj−1, θj, dj), like J. Craig. Khalil and Kleinfinger, and the course “Robot modelling” of this site, write (αj, dj, θj, rj): their dj is our distance aj−1, and their rj is our offset dj. The frames and the matrices are identical: only the letters change.

  • twist α
  • offset d
The essentials
  • Modified DH: frame j is on the axis of joint j, at the start of link j.
  • One row = α, a (along x, shape of the previous link), then θ, d (along z, motion of joint j).
  • A fixed last row often brings the frame to the tool.

8.3 The mistake that costs hours: mixing them

A standard table in a modified matrix gives another robot

Take the standard table of the 2-axis arm (a = 0.5 and 0.4, 30° and 45°) and feed it to the modified matrix by mistake. Each row now means “move forward, then turn” instead of “turn, then move forward”. The tool lands at (0.5 + 0.4 cos 30°, 0.4 sin 30°) = (0.846, 0.200) instead of (0.537, 0.636). No error message: just a robot that goes to the wrong place.

8.4 How to recognise the convention of a table

  • The columns start with θ, d and end with a, α → standard. They start with α, a → modified.
  • The indices ai−1, αi−1 appear → modified.
  • The letters are α, d, θ, r → modified, in the Khalil–Kleinfinger notation.
  • In a software library, look for an option such as “standard” / “modified” (often written mdh).

8.5 Checklist: is my table right?

  1. Zero pose: set every variable to 0, compute the tool position, and compare with the drawing of the robot in that pose.
  2. One joint at a time: turn joint 1 by 90° only. Does the tool go where you expect? Then joint 2, and so on.
  3. Parallel axes have α = 0; perpendicular axes have α = ±90°. Count them on the robot.
  4. Lengths: every non-zero a or d must be a real distance you can measure on the robot.
Which one should you use?

The one of your course, your book or your software, and only that one inside a project. Two people can also write two different correct tables in the same convention (the direction of an axis is a free choice): what counts is that the final matrix is right. On the robot pages of this site you can switch between the DH and DHM tables of the same robot and watch the frames move.

The essentials
  • Standard: frame at the far end of the link, row θ, d, a, α. Modified: frame on its own joint, row α, a, θ, d.
  • Both give the same tool position, if each table is used with its own matrix.
  • Always check a table with the zero pose.
