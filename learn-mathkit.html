Angles and trigonometry

10 min
You will learn

to measure an angle in degrees and radians, to use sine and cosine to find a point, and atan2 to find an angle.

1.1 Degrees and radians

A robot joint turns by an angle. We know degrees: a full turn is 360°. Computers and formulas use radians: a full turn is 2π ≈ 6.28 rad. So:

radians = degrees × π / 180    degrees = radians × 180 / π

Degrees0°30°45°90°180°360°
Radians0π/6 ≈ 0.52π/4 ≈ 0.79π/2 ≈ 1.57π ≈ 3.142π ≈ 6.28
Why radians?

With radians, the length of an arc is simply radius × angle. A wheel of radius 0.1 m that turns by 2 rad rolls 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 m. No factor to remember.

1.2 Sine and cosine: where is the end of the arm?

Take an arm of length L, fixed at the origin, tilted by an angle θ from the x axis. Its end is at:

x = L cos θ    y = L sin θ

The cosine gives the part of the arm along x, the sine the part along y. Values to know by heart:

θ0°30°45°60°90°180°
cos θ10.8660.7070.50−1
sin θ00.50.7070.86610
Worked example

An arm of 0.8 m at 30°: x = 0.8 × 0.866 = 0.693 m, y = 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4 m. Check with Pythagoras: √(0.693² + 0.4²) = 0.8 m, the length of the arm.

1.3 Two formulas you will meet everywhere

  • Pythagoras: cos² θ + sin² θ = 1. The distance from the origin to the point (x, y) is √(x² + y²).
  • Sum of angles: cos(a + b) = cos a cos b − sin a sin b, and sin(a + b) = sin a cos b + cos a sin b. This is why a 2-axis arm has terms in cos(θ1 + θ2).

1.4 The other way: from a point to an angle (atan2)

You know the point (x, y) and want the angle. Use θ = atan2(y, x), found in every programming language. Note the order: y first.

Never use arctan(y / x) alone

The points (1, 1) and (−1, −1) have the same ratio y/x = 1, so arctan gives 45° for both. But the second one is at −135°. atan2 looks at the signs of x and y and gives the right angle over the full turn, even when x = 0.

  • The end of an arm of length L at angle θ is at x = L cos θ, y = L sin θ.
  • To find an angle from a point, use atan2(y, x), never arctan(y/x) alone.

Vectors

11 min
You will learn

what a vector is, how to add vectors, measure their length, and use the dot and cross products.

2.1 An arrow with numbers

A vector is an arrow: it has a direction and a length. We write it as a column of numbers, its components: 31 means “3 to the right, 1 up”. In space there is a third number for the height: xyz.

In robotics vectors describe positions (where the tool is), speeds (how it moves), forces (how it pushes) and axes (around what a joint turns).

2.2 Adding vectors: one arrow after the other

To chain two moves, add the components one by one: 31 + 12 = 43.

This is a robot arm

Look at the picture again: the blue arrow is link 1, the orange arrow is link 2, and the dashed arrow goes from the base to the tool. The position of the tool is the sum of the arrows of the links. All of forward kinematics is in this sentence.

2.3 Length, scaling, unit vector

  • Length (norm): ‖v‖ = √(x² + y² + z²). For (4, 3): √(16 + 9) = 5.
  • Multiply by a number: every component is multiplied. 2 × (4, 3) = (8, 6): same direction, twice as long.
  • Unit vector: a vector of length 1, used to give a direction only. Divide a vector by its length: (4, 3) / 5 = (0.8, 0.6).

2.4 The dot product: how much two arrows agree

a · b = axbx + ayby + azbz = ‖a‖ ‖b‖ cos φ

The result is a number. φ is the angle between the two arrows. Three cases to remember:

The arrows are…Dot productExample
in the same directionpositive, maximum(1, 0) · (2, 0) = 2
perpendicularzero(1, 0) · (0, 3) = 0
oppositenegative(1, 0) · (−2, 0) = −2

Use: the dot product with a unit vector gives the part of a vector along that direction (the speed of a robot along its forward axis, the force along a joint axis).

2.5 The cross product: a vector perpendicular to two others

In 3D, a × b is a new vector, perpendicular to both a and b. Its direction is given by the right hand: index along a, middle finger along b, the thumb shows a × b.

x × y = z    y × z = x    z × x = y

  • It builds the third axis of a frame from the two others (lessons 5 and 6).
  • It gives the speed of a point that turns: v = ω × r, and the torque of a force: τ = r × F.

Matrices

12 min
You will learn

to multiply a matrix by a vector and by another matrix, and what the identity, the transpose, the inverse and the determinant are.

3.1 A table that transforms a vector

A matrix is a table of numbers. Its job: take a vector and give back another vector (turned, stretched, moved). The rule is “row times column”: multiply the numbers of a row by the numbers of the column, one by one, and add.

abcd xy = ax + bycx + dy

Worked example

1234 56 = 1×5 + 2×63×5 + 4×6 = 1739

What the columns mean

The first column of a matrix is where the vector 10 (the x axis) goes; the second column is where 01 (the y axis) goes. To build a matrix that does what you want, ask: “where do the axes go?” and write the answers as columns. Lesson 4 uses this to build the rotation matrix.

3.2 Matrix times matrix: two transformations in a row

The product A B is the matrix that does B first, then A. Same rule: the element in row i, column j of the result is row i of A “times” column j of B.

1234 0110 = 1×0 + 2×11×1 + 2×03×0 + 4×13×1 + 4×0 = 2143

The order matters

In general A B ≠ B A. With the same two matrices in the other order: 0110 1234 = 3412. It is normal: “turn then move forward” does not lead to the same place as “move forward then turn”.

Rotation matrices

12 min
You will learn

to build the matrix that turns a vector by an angle, its properties, and the three rotations of 3D space.

4.1 Build it from its columns

We want the matrix that turns every vector by an angle θ (counter-clockwise). Use the idea of lesson 3: where do the axes go?

  • The x axis 10 turns to cos θsin θ (lesson 1). This is the first column.
  • The y axis 01 turns to −sin θcos θ. This is the second column.
The rotation matrix in the plane

R(θ) = cos θ−sin θsin θcos θ

Worked example

Turn the vector 21 by 30° (cos = 0.866, sin = 0.5): 0.866−0.50.50.866 21 = 0.866×2 − 0.5×10.5×2 + 0.866×1 = 1.2321.866. The length did not change: √(2² + 1²) = √(1.232² + 1.866²) = 2.236.

4.3 Rotations in space

In 3D a rotation turns around an axis. The positive direction is given by the right hand: thumb along the axis, the fingers show the positive rotation. The three basic rotations keep one axis fixed (its row and column are 0, 0, 1):

Rz(θ) = c−s0sc0001 Ry(θ) = c0s010−s0c Rx(θ) = 1000c−s0sc

with c = cos θ and s = sin θ. Rz is the plane rotation of 4.1 with a third axis that does not move: it is the rotation of a revolute joint around its own z axis, the one you will meet in every DH table.

In 3D the order of rotations matters

Take the x axis (1, 0, 0). Turn it by 90° around x (nothing happens), then by 90° around z: it becomes (0, 1, 0). In the other order, 90° around z gives (0, 1, 0), then 90° around x gives (0, 0, 1). Two different results. In the plane the order did not matter; in space it does. Try it with a book in your hands.

Frames and transformations

14 min
You will learn

to go from one frame to another with a rotation and a translation, and to pack both in one homogeneous matrix that you can chain.

5.1 The same point, seen from two frames

A frame is an origin and axes: a place from which you measure. A robot has many: one on the floor, one on each link, one on the tool, one on the camera. The same point has different coordinates in each frame.

  • How to name a frame. A frame is an origin O and two perpendicular axes x and y (z in 3D). The small letter under each name says which frame it belongs to: OA, xA, yA are the axes of frame A; OB, xB, yB those of frame B. Red is always x, green is always y.
  • How to read the coordinates. Each axis is graduated in units (here 1 square = 1). For frame A, start at OA: P is 2 graduations along xA (to the right) and 3 along yA (up), so P = (2, 3) in A.
  • The same point for B. Frame B is turned by 90°: its xB axis points up and its yB axis points to the left. Start at OB: go 2 graduations along xB (up), then 1 along yB (to the left): you reach P. So P = (2, 1) in B.

Frame B sits at t = (3, 1) in frame A, and is turned by 90°. The point P is at (2, 1) for B. Where is it for A? Two steps: turn the coordinates with the rotation of B, then add the position of B.

Change of frame

pA = R pB + t

R = the orientation of B seen from A (its columns are the axes of B, written in A). t = the origin of B, written in A.

Worked example

R(90°) = 0−110. pA = 0−110 21 + 31 = −12 + 31 = 23. Check on the picture: from B, go 2 along xB (up) and 1 along yB (left).

5.2 One matrix for both: the homogeneous matrix

“Multiply, then add” is not practical to chain ten frames. The trick: add a 1 under every point, and put R and t in one bigger matrix. One product now does the rotation and the translation:

pA1 = Rt01 pB1    ATB = Rt01

In the plane it is a 3×3 matrix; in space a 4×4 matrix (R is 3×3, t has 3 numbers, the last row is always 0 0 0 1). With our example: 0−13101001 211 = 0×2 − 1×1 + 3×11×2 + 0×1 + 1×11 = 231.

How to read a homogeneous matrix
  • The last column is the position of the frame: where it is.
  • The other columns are its axes: how it is turned.
  • A point ends with 1 (the translation applies); a direction ends with 0 (it is only turned).

5.3 Chaining frames: this is forward kinematics

0T2 = 0T1 1T2

To go from frame 2 to frame 0, go through frame 1: multiply the matrices. Reading trick: the indices that touch “cancel” (the 1 below and the 1 above). For a robot with n joints: 0Tn = 0T1 1T2 … n−1Tn, and the last column of the result is the position of the tool.

Worked example: a 2-axis arm

L1 = 0.5 m, L2 = 0.4 m, θ1 = 30°, θ2 = 45°. Each matrix says “turn by θ, then move forward by L along the new x”: its last column is (L cos θ, L sin θ).

0T1 = 0.866−0.50.4330.50.8660.25001 1T2 = 0.707−0.7070.2830.7070.7070.283001 0T2 = 0.259−0.9660.5370.9660.2590.636001

The tool is at x = 0.537 m, y = 0.636 m. The same as the sum of arrows of lesson 2: 0.5 cos 30° + 0.4 cos 75° = 0.537 and 0.5 sin 30° + 0.4 sin 75° = 0.636. And the top-left block is a rotation of 75° = 30° + 45°: the orientation of the tool.

5.4 Going back: the inverse

(ATB)−1 = BTA = RT−RT t01

No heavy computation: transpose the rotation, and turn the translation back. It answers the opposite question: “where is A, seen from B?”

6

The Denavit–Hartenberg method (standard DH)

25 min
You will learn

to draw the axes z and x of every frame step by step, to know exactly between which two axes each parameter is measured, and to fill the DH table of any robot alone.

6.1 The problem: too much freedom

Lesson 5 showed that a robot is a chain of frames, one per link, and that we multiply their matrices. If everybody places the frames freely, each frame needs 6 numbers and nobody gets the same equations. Denavit and Hartenberg (1955) gave rules to place the frames; with them, going from one frame to the next needs only 4 numbers, and a whole robot becomes a small table: one row per joint.

6.2 Numbering: links, joints, frames

  • Links (the rigid parts): 0 = the fixed base, 1, 2, … n = the last link, which carries the tool.
  • Joints: joint i connects link i−1 to link i. Its axis is the axis of rotation (revolute joint, R) or the direction of sliding (prismatic joint, P).
  • Frames: frame i (origin Oi, axes xi, yi, zi) is fixed to link i. Frame 0 is fixed to the base and never moves.

6.3 The method: how to draw the axes

Draw the robot in a simple pose (often its zero pose), then place the axes in this order: all the z first, then all the x, then the origins. The y axes are never drawn.

Standard DH: drawing the frames, step by step
  1. Step 1, the z axes. Put zi−1 on the axis of joint i: z0 on joint 1, z1 on joint 2, …, zn−1 on joint n. Choose its direction (either way is correct): the positive rotation of the joint is then given by the right-hand rule around it.
  2. Step 2, the x axes. For i = 1 to n, look at the two axes zi−1 and zi, and put xi on their common perpendicular (the shortest segment that joins them, perpendicular to both), pointing from zi−1 towards zi.
  3. Step 3, the origins. Oi is the point where xi meets zi.
  4. Step 4, frame 0 (base). O0 anywhere on z0 (often on the floor), x0 perpendicular to z0, in a direction you choose — usually the direction of x1 in the drawn pose, so that θ1 = 0 there.
  5. Step 5, frame n (tool). There is no joint n+1: put On on the tool point, zn parallel to zn−1 (or along the direction in which the tool points), and xn from zn−1 towards the tool.

The modified DH method (DHM)

22 min
You will learn

7.1 One idea changes: the frame sits on its own joint

In standard DH, frame i−1 is on joint i: the numbers are shifted. The modified convention (Khalil and Kleinfinger, 1986; also J. Craig) puts frame j on joint j, the joint that moves link j: the same number everywhere. It is the convention of most French courses.

7.2 The method: how to draw the axes

Modified DH: drawing the frames, step by step
  1. Step 1, the z axes. Put zj on the axis of joint j: z1 on joint 1, z2 on joint 2, …, zn on joint n. Choose its direction.
  2. Step 2, the x axes. For j = 1 to n−1, put xj on the common perpendicular to zj and zj+1, pointing from zj towards zj+1. The four cases of lesson 6 (skew, crossing, parallel, same axis) apply the same way.
  3. Step 3, the origins. Oj is the point where xj meets zj — so the origin of frame j is on its own joint.
  4. Step 4, frame 0 (base). Usually z0 on the same line as z1, and x0 in the direction of x1 when q1 = 0: row 1 is then as simple as possible.
  5. Step 5, the last joint and the tool. There is no zn+1: choose xn (usually towards the tool). The tool gets its own frame E, linked to frame n by one more fixed row (no variable).

Derivatives and partial derivatives: from positions to speeds

15 min
You will learn

what a derivative is, the few rules you need, how a computer estimates it, and how partial derivatives build the Jacobian of a robot.

9.1 A derivative is a rate of change

When the position x of something changes with the time t, its derivative dx/dt says how fast it changes: it is the speed. On the graph of x(t), it is the slope of the curve at that moment. In robotics we write it with a dot: ẋ = dx/dt, θ̇ = dθ/dt.

The derivative of the derivative is the acceleration: ẍ = d²x/dt². Position → speed → acceleration: each one is the rate of change of the one before.

Worked example: the speed of the end of an arm

The end of an arm of length L is at x = L cos θ, y = L sin θ (lesson 1). The motor turns, so θ depends on time. Chain rule:

ẋ = −L sin θ · θ̇    ẏ = L cos θ · θ̇

With L = 0.5 m, θ = 30° and θ̇ = 2 rad/s: ẋ = −0.5 × 0.5 × 2 = −0.5 m/s, ẏ = 0.5 × 0.866 × 2 = 0.866 m/s. The speed is L θ̇ = 1 m/s, perpendicular to the arm, as expected for a point that turns.

9.3 How a computer derives: the finite difference

A robot does not have the formula of its motion: it has measurements at regular times. It estimates the derivative with the change divided by the time step:

θ̇ ≈ ( θ(t + Δt) − θ(t) ) / Δt

Example: the encoder reads 1.00 rad, then 1.02 rad 10 ms later: θ̇ ≈ 0.02 / 0.01 = 2 rad/s.

Deriving amplifies noise

If the encoder has a noise of 0.001 rad, dividing by Δt = 0.001 s gives a speed noise of about 1 rad/s. This is why the D term of a PID is always filtered (control course), and why speeds are often estimated with a filter rather than a raw difference.

9.4 Partial derivatives: one variable at a time

The tool of a 2-axis arm depends on two angles: x = L1 cos θ1 + L2 cos(θ1 + θ2). The partial derivative ∂x/∂θ1 is the derivative with respect to θ1, the other angle being frozen. It answers: “if only motor 1 turns a little, how much does x change?”

∂x/∂θ1 = −L1 sin θ1 − L2 sin(θ1 + θ2)    ∂x/∂θ2 = −L2 sin(θ1 + θ2)

∂y/∂θ1 = L1 cos θ1 + L2 cos(θ1 + θ2)    ∂y/∂θ2 = L2 cos(θ1 + θ2)

Look at ∂x/∂θ2: the first term L1 cos θ1 disappeared, because it does not contain θ2. Motor 2 only moves the second link.

9.5 The Jacobian: the table of all partial derivatives

Worked example

Two uses you will meet again

Probability: living with noisy sensors

14 min
You will learn

the mean, the variance and the standard deviation, the normal law and its 68–95–99.7 rule, and how to combine measurements: the idea behind the Kalman filter.

10.1 Every measurement is a little wrong

Measure the same wall five times with a laser: 2.02 m, 1.98 m, 2.05 m, 1.97 m, 1.98 m. None is “the” distance. A robot must decide with numbers like these. Probability gives the tools to say what is most likely and how sure we are.

10.2 Mean, variance, standard deviation

QuantityFormulaMeaningOur 5 measurements
Mean μ(x1 + … + xN) / Nthe best guess10.00 / 5 = 2.00 m
Variance σ²mean of (x − μ)²the spread, in m²(0.0004 + 0.0004 + 0.0025 + 0.0009 + 0.0004) / 5 = 0.00092 m²
Standard deviation σ√(σ²)the spread, in metres: the typical error√0.00092 ≈ 0.03 m (3 cm)

(Statistics books often divide by N − 1 instead of N for a small sample: here it gives 0.034 m. The idea is the same.)

10.3 The normal law (the bell curve)

The noise of most sensors follows the normal (Gaussian) law: values near the mean are frequent, far values are rare. It is described by only two numbers, μ and σ. The rule to remember:

IntervalShare of the measurements
μ ± σ68 %
μ ± 2σ95 %
μ ± 3σ99.7 %
Worked example

A lidar has σ = 2 cm and reads 1.50 m. With 95 % confidence the true distance is in 1.50 ± 0.04 m, between 1.46 m and 1.54 m. A safety stop that must never miss an obstacle uses ±3σ: 1.44 m.

10.4 Three rules to handle errors

  1. Averaging reduces the noise: the mean of N independent measurements has a standard deviation σ / √N. Four measurements divide the error by 2; one hundred divide it by 10.
  2. Independent errors add in variance, not in standard deviation: σtotal = √(σ1² + σ2²). A camera error of 3 mm and a robot error of 4 mm give 5 mm, not 7 mm.
  3. Independent events multiply: if a grasp succeeds 9 times out of 10, three grasps in a row succeed 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 73 % of the time.

10.5 Combining two sensors: Kalman in one line

Two sensors measure the same position: the GPS says 10 m (σ = 3 m), the wheels say 12 m (σ = 4 m). The best combination gives each one a weight 1/σ²: the more precise sensor counts more.

x = (x1/σ1² + x2/σ2²) / (1/σ1² + 1/σ2²)    1/σ² = 1/σ1² + 1/σ2²

Here: weights 1/9 = 0.111 and 1/16 = 0.0625, so x = (10 × 0.111 + 12 × 0.0625) / 0.174 = 10.72 m, and 1/σ² = 0.174, so σ = 2.4 m: better than each sensor alone. The Kalman filter repeats this fusion at every step, between the prediction of the motion and the new measurement.

What to remember

A measurement without its σ is half a measurement. Robots carry, with every estimate, its uncertainty: this is what lets them combine sensors, decide when to trust them, and stop safely.

You will learn

the four ways to describe how an object is turned in space, why robots and ROS 2 use quaternions, and how to read and write one.

11.1 Four ways to say “how it is turned”

A position needs 3 numbers. An orientation in space also has 3 degrees of freedom, but there are several ways to write it, each with its strengths:

RepresentationNumbersStrengthWeakness
Rotation matrix (lesson 4)9direct to compute with: chain by multiplying9 numbers for 3 degrees of freedom; small errors make it slowly stop being a rotation
Euler / roll-pitch-yaw angles3easy for a human to readgimbal lock; 12 different conventions; bad for interpolation
Axis-angle3 (or 4)easy to picture: one axis, one anglecomposing two rotations is not simple
Quaternion4no gimbal lock, compact, smooth interpolation, cheap to fixnot intuitive: you read it through formulas

11.2 Roll, pitch, yaw — and their trap

For a drone, a car or a gripper we often say: yaw ψ (turn around the vertical z), pitch θ (nose up or down, around y), roll φ (tilt the wings, around x). In the usual convention:

R = Rz(ψ) Ry(θ) Rx(φ)

Gimbal lock

When the pitch reaches ±90° (nose straight up), the yaw axis and the roll axis line up: two of the three angles do the same thing, and one degree of freedom is lost in the description (not in the object). Near this pose, a small motion of the object can make the angles jump. The course “Singularities and the Jacobian” (lesson 6) shows it in 3D.

11.3 Axis-angle: one axis, one angle

A theorem of Euler says: any orientation can be reached by one single rotation, by an angle θ, around one well-chosen axis u (a unit vector). Hold a book, turn it any way you like: there is always one axis that would have done it in one move.

Packed as one vector θ u it is called the rotation vector: its direction is the axis, its length the angle. The angle can be read directly on a rotation matrix:

cos θ = ( trace R − 1 ) / 2    (trace = R11 + R22 + R33)

Check: Rz(90°) = 0−10100001 has a trace of 0 + 0 + 1 = 1, so cos θ = 0 and θ = 90°.

11.4 The quaternion: axis-angle in a smart form

A unit quaternion is four numbers built from the axis and the angle, using half the angle:

The quaternion of a rotation by θ around u

q = ( w, x, y, z ) = ( cos θ/2, ux sin θ/2, uy sin θ/2, uz sin θ/2 )

Its length is always 1: w² + x² + y² + z² = 1.

RotationQuaternion (w, x, y, z)
none(1, 0, 0, 0)
90° around z(0.707, 0, 0, 0.707)
120° around x(0.5, 0.866, 0, 0)
180° around y(0, 0, 1, 0)

Three rules are enough to use them:

  • Chain two rotations: multiply the quaternions, q = q1 q2 (as for matrices, the order matters). The product of (w1, v1) and (w2, v2) is (w1w2 − v1·v2, w1v2 + w2v1 + v1 × v2), with the dot and cross products of lesson 2.
  • Undo a rotation: change the sign of x, y, z (the conjugate q* = (w, −x, −y, −z)).
  • Turn a vector v: write it as (0, v) and compute q (0, v) q*. In practice a library does it, or converts q to a rotation matrix.
Worked example: 45° then 45° around z

q45 = (cos 22.5°, 0, 0, sin 22.5°) = (0.924, 0, 0, 0.383). Product with itself: w = 0.924² − 0.383² = 0.707, z = 2 × 0.924 × 0.383 = 0.707. We get (0.707, 0, 0, 0.707): the quaternion of 90° around z, as expected.

q and −q are the same orientation

Turning by θ around u or by 360° − θ around −u ends in the same place, and the formula gives q and −q. Do not be surprised if a program prints (−0.707, 0, 0, −0.707) for 90° around z.

11.5 Quaternions in a robot program

A mobile robot on a flat floor only turns around z by its heading ψ (yaw). Then the quaternion is simple, and the heading comes back with atan2:

q = ( cos ψ/2, 0, 0, sin ψ/2 )    ψ = 2 atan2(z, w)

For a general orientation: ψ = atan2( 2(wz + xy), 1 − 2(y² + z²) ).

The order of the four numbers differs between libraries
  • ROS 2 messages (geometry_msgs/Quaternion), tf2 and SciPy write (x, y, z, w): w last.
  • Eigen (C++), many books and this lesson write (w, x, y, z): w first.
  • A heading of 90° is (0, 0, 0.707, 0.707) in a ROS message. Mixing the two orders gives a robot that faces the wrong way, with no error message.

11.6 Why robots prefer quaternions

  1. No gimbal lock: every orientation has a smooth description.
  2. Compact and cheap: 4 numbers instead of 9; after many products, dividing by the length is enough to correct the rounding errors.
  3. Smooth interpolation: the slerp goes from one orientation to another at constant speed, along the shortest path. Halfway between 0° and 90° around z it gives exactly 45°. This is how a robot tool turns smoothly during a linear move, and how an IMU filter tracks the attitude of a drone.
In practice

Store and compute orientations as quaternions, show them to humans as roll, pitch, yaw, and convert to a rotation matrix when you need to turn many points. Let a library do the conversions, and check its order (w first or last).
