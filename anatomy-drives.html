Anatomy of the drive configurations (3D)

Turn the robot with the mouse or your finger. Click an axis (a coloured circle or a card) to light up its ring and its axis and to read its description, press X-ray to look inside at the motors, gearboxes, controller and cables, and move each joint with its slider.

Definition

A drive configuration is the way the wheels of a mobile robot are arranged, driven and steered. It fixes how the robot can move: a differential drive turns by driving its two wheels at different speeds, a car steers its front wheels, and omnidirectional drives can move in any direction at once.

Command of the robot

Choose how the robot body must move; the wheels follow. Then pick a configuration below to compare.

(δm, δs) = (2, 0). Two driven fixed wheels and a castor. Turns by driving the wheels at different speeds. Cannot slide sideways: not holonomic.

(δm, δs) = (1, 1). One steered and driven front wheel, two fixed rear wheels. Tow tractors and AGV forklifts. Not holonomic.

(δm, δs) = (1, 1). Two steered front wheels, two driven rear wheels. The inner wheel turns more than the outer one. Cannot turn on the spot.

(δm, δs) = (1, 1). Three wheels, all steered and driven together by a common mechanism: the robot goes in any direction without turning its body.

(δm, δs) = (3, 0). Three omni wheels at 120°. Holonomic: any motion, instantly. Used in RoboCup and small platforms.

(δm, δs) = (3, 0). Four Mecanum wheels in an X layout. Holonomic: sideways motion comes from the wheel speed combination. Needs a flat floor.

(δm, δs) = (2, 1). Four wheels, each steered and driven. Holonomic once the wheels have turned. Competition robots and heavy AGVs.

(δm, δs) = (2, 0). Two tracks (or four fixed wheels) that turn by slipping on the ground. Rugged but the slipping makes the odometry poor.

What is inside each joint?

  • Wheel motors: each driven wheel has a motor and a gearbox (in the hub or beside it) and an encoder; the controller computes every wheel speed from the commanded body motion (inverse kinematics).
  • Steering motors (seen in X-ray on the steered wheels): a second motor and a slip ring per wheel; they make swerve modules heavy and expensive.
  • Rollers of omni and Mecanum wheels: free rollers that let a wheel slide in one direction while it drives in the other.
  • Suspension and floor: three wheels always touch the floor; four wheels need a suspension (or a rocker) so that all of them keep their grip.

The wheel speeds are computed live from the three commands (vx forward, vy to the left, ω turning left) with the inverse kinematics of each configuration, and the robot drives for real; the orange line is its path. A configuration that cannot do a motion (a sideways slide, for example) ignores it and says so. The model is simplified: wheel radius 0.07 m, see the wheels page for the equations.
