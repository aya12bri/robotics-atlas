Parallel robots: the Delta and the Stewart platform

20 min
You will learn

why closed chains change everything, how to compute the inverse kinematics of a Delta and of a Stewart platform, why their forward kinematics is hard, and their two kinds of singularities.

1.1 Serial and parallel: the world turned upside down

In a serial robot the links form one chain from the base to the tool. In a parallel robot the tool (the moving platform) is held by several chains at once, each with its own motor near the base. The motors do not carry each other: the moving parts are light, so the robot is very fast (Delta: up to 300 picks per minute) and very stiff (Stewart: flight simulators, machine tools).

Serial robotParallel robot
Forward kinematicseasy: multiply the matriceshard: a system of equations with several solutions
Inverse kinematicshard: several solutions, often numericaleasy: one independent formula per chain
Workspacelargesmall (the intersection of the chains)
Stiffness, speederrors add along the chainthe chains share the load: stiff and fast

1.2 The Delta robot: inverse kinematics, arm by arm

Three motors on the base, at 120° from each other, at a radius R. Each turns an upper arm of length L; a parallelogram of length l links its end to the platform, at a radius r from its centre. The parallelograms keep the platform horizontal: it only translates (3 DOF: x, y, z).

The key fact: each arm only sees the point where its forearm meets the platform. Given the platform position, this point is known, and the motor angle θ must place the elbow at distance l from it. In the plane of the arm (x along the arm, z up), with (xq, yq, zq) the wrist joint seen from the motor axis:

k = (xq² + yq² + zq² + L² − l²) / (2L)    ρ = √(xq² + zq²)    θ = −atan2(zq, xq) − arccos(k / ρ)

It comes from writing |elbow − wrist|² = l² and grouping the terms in cos θ and sin θ. If |k| > ρ, the point is out of reach. The second sign of arccos gives the elbow pointing inwards, which is never used.

"""Inverse kinematics of a Delta robot: one closed formula per arm, then a check of every constraint."""
import numpy as np

R, r = 0.20, 0.05          # radius of the motor circle (base) and of the joint circle (moving platform), m
L, l = 0.30, 0.60          # upper arm (driven by the motor) and forearm (the parallelogram), m
PHI = np.radians([0, 120, 240])   # directions of the three arms around the vertical axis


def ik(p):
    """Platform centre p = (x, y, z) -> the three motor angles (rad, positive = arm going down), or None."""
    angles = []
    for phi in PHI:
        c, s = np.cos(phi), np.sin(phi)
        x, y, z = c * p[0] + s * p[1], -s * p[0] + c * p[1], p[2]   # p seen from the plane of this arm
        xq, yq, zq = x + r - R, y, z                                  # wrist joint relative to the motor axis
        k = (xq**2 + yq**2 + zq**2 + L**2 - l**2) / (2 * L)
        rho = np.hypot(xq, zq)
        if abs(k) > rho:
            return None                                              # out of the workspace
        angles.append(-np.arctan2(zq, xq) - np.arccos(k / rho))      # the elbow pointing outwards
    return np.array(angles)


def check(p, th):
    """Length of each forearm for the solution: must be exactly l."""
    out = []
    for phi, t in zip(PHI, th):
        c, s = np.cos(phi), np.sin(phi)
        elbow = np.array([R + L * np.cos(t), 0, -L * np.sin(t)])
        wrist = np.array([c * p[0] + s * p[1] + r, -s * p[0] + c * p[1], p[2]])
        out.append(np.linalg.norm(elbow - wrist))
    return np.array(out)


if __name__ == '__main__':
    np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)
    for p in [(0, 0, -0.6), (0.1, 0, -0.6), (0.05, 0.08, -0.55), (0.6, 0, -0.6)]:
        th = ik(np.array(p, dtype=float))
        if th is None:

        else:

1.3 The Stewart platform: six legs, six lengths

Six linear actuators (legs) link the base to a platform that can move in all 6 directions. Its inverse kinematics is the simplest of all robots: for a wanted pose (position p, rotation R) of the platform, leg i joins the base point ai to the platform point bi, so its length is:

ℓi = ‖ p + R bi − ai ‖

Example: a = (0.5, 0, 0), b = (0.3, 0, 0), platform at p = (0, 0, 0.8) without rotation: ℓ = ‖(−0.2, 0, 0.8)‖ = 0.825 m. Six such lines, and the controller knows the length to give each leg. The forward problem (six lengths → the pose) can have up to 40 solutions and is solved numerically, starting from the last known pose.

1.4 Two kinds of singularities

KindWhat happensExample
Serial (type 1)a chain is stretched or folded: the platform loses a direction of motion, as in a serial armthe edge of the workspace
Parallel (type 2)the platform gains a free motion even with the motors locked: it can no longer resist a force in that directionlegs of a Stewart platform all lying in one plane
Type 2 is dangerous

Near a parallel singularity the motor torques needed to hold a small load become huge, and the robot can collapse or break. Designers choose the dimensions so that these poses lie outside the useful workspace, and the controller checks the distance to them.

Optimal control: the LQR

18 min
You will learn

what “optimal” means for a controller, how the LQR finds the best gains from two weights, and how to choose them.

2.1 From tuning to a price

A PID is tuned by hand, gain by gain. With many states (positions and speeds of 6 joints, a drone, a balancing robot) this becomes impossible. The linear quadratic regulator (LQR) asks a different question: what does an error cost, and what does a command cost? Then it computes the gains that make the total cost as small as possible.

For a system in state-space form ẋ = A x + B u (control course, lesson 11):

J = ∫ ( xT Q x + uT R u ) dt

  • Q (a diagonal matrix) = the price of each error: a large Q on the position means “be accurate”.
  • R = the price of the command: a large R means “save the motors, avoid big torques”.
The result

The best controller is always a simple state feedback u = −K x, with K = R−1 BT P, where P solves the Riccati equation:

AT P + P A − P B R−1 BT P + Q = 0

You never solve it by hand: every library has a function (scipy.linalg.solve_continuous_are, lqr in MATLAB). The closed loop is always stable when the system is controllable.

2.2 A joint, by LQR

A joint driven in acceleration is a double integrator: state = (position error, speed), ẍ = u. The code below integrates the Riccati equation until it stops changing, for three prices of the position error:

"""LQR for a joint seen as a double integrator: find the best gains K for u = -K x."""
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [0.0, 0.0]])     # x = (position error, speed), x' = A x + B u
B = np.array([[0.0], [1.0]])               # u = acceleration
R = np.array([[1.0]])                      # price of the command

for q in [1.0, 100.0, 10000.0]:            # price of the position error
    Q = np.diag([q, 0.0])
    P = np.eye(2)
    for _ in range(200000):                # integrate the Riccati equation until it stops changing
        dP = A.T @ P + P @ A - P @ B @ np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P + Q
        P += 1e-4 * dP
        if np.abs(dP).max() < 1e-9:
            break
    K = np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P
    poles = np.linalg.eigvals(A - B @ K)
    wn = abs(poles[0]); zeta = -poles[0].real / wn
    print(f'q = {q:7.0f}:  K = [{K[0, 0]:7.3f} {K[0, 1]:6.3f}]   poles = {np.round(poles, 3)}   '
          f'wn = {wn:5.2f} rad/s   damping = {zeta:.3f}')
q =       1:  K = [  1.000  1.414]   poles = [-0.707+0.707j -0.707-0.707j]   wn =  1.00 rad/s   damping = 0.707
q =     100:  K = [ 10.000  4.472]   poles = [-2.236+2.236j -2.236-2.236j]   wn =  3.16 rad/s   damping = 0.707
q =   10000:  K = [100.000 14.142]   poles = [-7.071+7.071j -7.071-7.071j]   wn = 10.00 rad/s   damping = 0.707
What it teaches
  • The LQR finds a PD controller: K = [Kp, Kd]. For this system, in theory Kp = √(q/r) and Kd = √(2 √(q/r)): 10 and 4.47 for q = 100, as computed.
  • Multiplying q by 100 makes the loop 10 times faster (ωn = 1 → 10 rad/s) and asks for 100 times more gain.
  • The damping is always 0.707: the LQR gives a well-damped loop by itself, with a good phase margin (at least 60° in theory).

2.3 How to choose Q and R

  1. Bryson's rule: put on the diagonal 1 / (largest acceptable value)². If 1 cm of error is the most you accept, Q = 1 / 0.01² = 10 000; if the motor gives at most 5 N·m, R = 1 / 25.
  2. Simulate, look at the largest command; if it is too big, increase R; if the response is too slow, increase Q.
  3. The LQR needs the full state: if only positions are measured, an observer (a Kalman filter) estimates the speeds. Together they are called LQG.

Where it is used: balancing robots and Segways, the attitude of drones and satellites, the stabilisation of legged robots around a pose, and as the inner loop of many industrial controllers.

Key terms

  • LQR
  • cost function
  • Riccati equation
  • state feedback
  • Bryson's rule
  • LQG
The essentials
  • LQR: minimise ∫ (xᵀQx + uᵀRu) dt; Q = price of the errors, R = price of the commands.
  • The optimal controller is u = −K x, K = R⁻¹ Bᵀ P with P from the Riccati equation.
  • Bryson's rule: 1 / (largest acceptable value)² on the diagonal.

Model predictive control (MPC)

18 min
You will learn

how a controller can look into the future, optimise a whole sequence of commands, respect the limits of the motors, and why it has become the standard for legged robots, cars and drones.

3.1 Plan, act a little, plan again

A driver looks at the road ahead, plans how to take the next bend, turns the wheel a little, looks again, and corrects the plan. MPC does exactly this, many times per second:

  1. Predict: with the model xk+1 = A xk + B uk, compute where the robot will be over the next N steps (the horizon) for any sequence of commands.
  2. Optimise: choose the sequence u0 … uN−1 that minimises a cost (errors and commands, as in the LQR) while respecting the limits (torque, speed, obstacles).
  3. Apply only the first command u0, measure the new state, and start again at the next sample.
The big advantage: constraints

A PID or an LQR does not know the limits: when the motor saturates, the loop behaves badly (lesson 12 of the control course and its anti-windup). MPC plans with the limits: it knows that the motor cannot give more than 1 rad/s², so it starts braking at the right time.

3.2 A joint with a limited acceleration

The joint must go from 0 to 1 rad and stop, with |u| ≤ 1 rad/s², a sample time of 0.1 s and a horizon of 20 steps (2 s). The predictions are stacked into one big matrix equation; the optimisation is a quadratic programme, solved here with a simple projected gradient (descend, then clip to the limits):

"""Model predictive control of a joint (double integrator) with a limit on the acceleration."""
import numpy as np

dt, N = 0.1, 20                   # sample time (s) and horizon (steps): the controller looks 2 s ahead
A = np.array([[1, dt], [0, 1]])   # state x = (position, speed)
B = np.array([[0.5 * dt**2], [dt]])
U_MAX = 1.0                       # |acceleration| <= 1 rad/s^2 (the motor limit)
Q, R_W = np.diag([10.0, 1.0]), 0.1


def predict_matrices():
    """Stack the predictions: X = F x0 + G U over the whole horizon."""
    F = np.vstack([np.linalg.matrix_power(A, k + 1) for k in range(N)])
    G = np.zeros((2 * N, N))
    for k in range(N):
        for j in range(k + 1):
            G[2 * k:2 * k + 2, j:j + 1] = np.linalg.matrix_power(A, k - j) @ B
    return F, G


F, G = predict_matrices()
Qbar = np.kron(np.eye(N), Q)
H = G.T @ Qbar @ G + R_W * np.eye(N)          # cost = U^T H U + 2 U^T g  (a quadratic programme)


def mpc_step(x0, target):
    g = G.T @ Qbar @ (F @ x0 - np.tile(target, N))
    U = np.zeros(N)
    step = 1.0 / np.linalg.eigvalsh(H).max()
    for _ in range(300):                       # projected gradient: descend, then clip to the limits
        U = np.clip(U - step * (H @ U + g), -U_MAX, U_MAX)
    return U[0]                                # apply only the first command, then do it all again


if __name__ == '__main__':
    x, target = np.array([0.0, 0.0]), np.array([1.0, 0.0])    # go from 0 to 1 rad and stop
    for k in range(41):
        u = mpc_step(x, target)
        if k % 5 == 0:

Legged robots: balance, ZMP and the inverted pendulum

18 min
You will learn

when a legged robot is stable, what the zero moment point is, how the linear inverted pendulum model explains walking, and where to put the foot to stop after a push.

4.1 Static balance: the support polygon

Standing still, a robot is stable if the vertical through its centre of mass falls inside the support polygon: the convex area drawn by the feet on the ground. A table with four legs has a large polygon; a biped on one foot has a polygon the size of its sole. Slow quadruped gaits keep three feet on the ground and move the body so that this condition always holds.

4.2 Dynamic balance: the ZMP

When the robot moves, inertia adds to gravity. The zero moment point (ZMP) is the point of the ground where the total of gravity and inertia forces has no tipping moment: it is where the ground reaction acts. The rule of all walking controllers since the 1970s: keep the ZMP inside the support polygon, and the feet stay flat on the ground.

4.3 The linear inverted pendulum

Model the robot as a point mass at a constant height zc above the ground, on a massless leg whose foot is at the ZMP p. The horizontal motion of the centre of mass x becomes linear:

ẍ = ω² ( x − p )    ω = √( g / zc )

If the centre of mass is ahead of the foot, it accelerates forward: the robot falls, exponentially, with the time constant 1/ω. For zc = 0.8 m: ω = 3.50 rad/s, 1/ω = 0.29 s. Walking is a controlled fall: the robot falls forward and puts its next foot to catch itself, step after step.

4.4 The capture point: where to step

Combine position and speed into one number, the capture point (or divergent component of motion):

ξ = x + ẋ / ω

If the robot puts its foot exactly at ξ, it comes to a stop above it. Example: after a push the centre of mass, above the foot, moves at 0.5 m/s: ξ = 0.5 / 3.50 = 0.14 m. A step of 14 cm stops the robot; a shorter step does not, and it must step again. This is the logic of push recovery in modern humanoids.

4.5 From the model to a walking robot

  1. Footstep planner: where to put the feet (on a map, around obstacles).
  2. Centre of mass planner: an MPC on the inverted pendulum (lesson 3) keeps the ZMP in the feet while following the steps.
  3. Whole-body controller: turns the wanted motion of the centre of mass and of the feet into joint torques, with the full dynamic model (control course, lesson 10) and the contact forces.
  4. State estimation: IMU + joint encoders + contact detection give the pose of the body (a Kalman filter).

Quadrupeds use the same ideas with trotting gaits (two diagonal feet at once). Recent robots also learn their gaits in simulation with reinforcement learning (lesson 7), with the model-based layers as a safety net.

5

Aerial robots: how a quadrotor flies and is controlled

16 min
You will learn

the forces on a quadrotor, why it is underactuated, how four motor speeds become thrust and torques, and the cascade of controllers that keeps it in the air.

5.1 Forces and the hover

Each propeller gives a thrust fi = kf ωi² (proportional to the square of its speed) and a drag torque km ωi². Two propellers turn clockwise and two anticlockwise, so their drag torques cancel. To hover, the total thrust equals the weight: T = m g. For a 1.2 kg drone: T = 11.8 N, so 2.94 N per motor.

5.2 Four motors, six degrees of freedom

A quadrotor moves in 6 directions (3 positions, 3 angles) with only 4 inputs: it is underactuated. It cannot push sideways; to move forward it must tilt, so that part of the thrust points forward. With a tilt φ at constant altitude, the horizontal acceleration is a = g tan φ: 10° gives 1.73 m/s².

5.3 The mixer

The flight controller asks for a total thrust T and three torques (roll τφ, pitch τθ, yaw τψ). For an X frame of arm length d, the four thrusts follow from one 4×4 matrix, inverted once:

To…Do
climball four motors faster
roll rightleft motors faster, right motors slower
pitch forwardrear motors faster, front motors slower
turn (yaw)the pair turning one way faster, the other pair slower: the drag torques no longer cancel

5.4 The cascade of controllers

  1. Position loop (10 to 50 Hz): position error → wanted acceleration → wanted tilt angles and thrust.
  2. Attitude loop (250 Hz to 1 kHz): angle error → wanted angular speeds.
  3. Rate loop (1 to 8 kHz): gyroscope → torques → mixer → motor speeds (through the ESCs).
Why a cascade

Each inner loop is 5 to 10 times faster than the one around it (control course, lesson 8). The outer loop can then consider that its order is executed at once. The attitude is estimated by fusing the gyroscope (fast, drifts) and the accelerometer (slow, noisy) with a complementary or Kalman filter, as quaternions (maths course, lesson 11).

Open-source autopilots (PX4, ArduPilot, Betaflight) implement exactly this structure and talk to ROS 2. Aggressive flight (racing, perching) replaces the outer loops by MPC on the full model.

Key terms

  • thrust
  • underactuated
  • mixer
  • attitude
  • cascade control
  • ESC
The essentials
  • Hover: total thrust = m g, shared by the 4 motors; thrust ∝ (speed of the propeller)².
  • Underactuated: to move, the drone tilts; a = g tan φ.
  • Cascade: position (slow) → attitude → angular rate (fast) → mixer → motors.

Vision in practice: calibration, markers, hand-eye and point clouds

18 min
You will learn

the full chain that lets a robot grasp what a camera sees: intrinsic calibration, pose from markers, hand-eye calibration, and depth images turned into 3D points.

6.1 The camera matrix

A camera projects a point (X, Y, Z), expressed in the camera frame, to the pixel (u, v):

u = fx X/Z + cx    v = fy Y/Z + cy    K = fx0cx0fycy001

f (in pixels) and the image centre (cx, cy) are the intrinsic parameters, found once by calibration: film a printed checkerboard from 15 to 30 angles, and a library (OpenCV calibrateCamera) finds K and the lens distortion. A good calibration has a reprojection error below 0.5 pixel.

6.2 Pose from a marker

An ArUco or AprilTag marker is a black and white square with a known size and a code. The camera finds its four corners in the image; since their 3D positions on the marker are known, solving the PnP problem (Perspective-n-Point, OpenCV solvePnP) gives the full pose of the marker in the camera frame: CTmarker. Markers are used to calibrate, to localise robots, and to find workpieces.

6.3 Hand-eye calibration

The camera sees the object; the robot must grasp it. We need the transform between the camera and the robot. Two set-ups:

Set-upUnknownChain used to grasp
Eye-in-hand: camera on the tooltoolTcamerabaseTobj = baseTtool · toolTcam · camTobj
Eye-to-hand: fixed camerabaseTcamerabaseTobj = baseTcam · camTobj

To find the unknown X, move the robot to 10 to 20 poses while the camera looks at a fixed marker. Between two poses, the motion of the tool A (from the robot) and the motion seen by the camera B satisfy A X = X B, which OpenCV (calibrateHandEye) solves. This is exactly the chain of homogeneous matrices of the maths course, lesson 5.

6.4 Depth images and point clouds

A depth camera (stereo, structured light, time of flight) gives a distance Z for every pixel. Invert the projection to get a 3D point per pixel: a point cloud.

X = (u − cx) Z / fx    Y = (v − cy) Z / fy

Example: f = 600 px, cx = 320, a pixel at u = 420 with a depth of 0.5 m: X = 100 × 0.5 / 600 = 8.3 cm to the right of the optical axis.

A typical bin-picking pipeline
  1. Point cloud from the depth camera; keep only the points inside the bin (crop).
  2. Remove the floor or the table: fit a plane with RANSAC (try random planes through 3 points, keep the one that fits the most points).
  3. Split the rest into objects (clustering), or detect them in the colour image with a neural network.
  4. Compute a grasp pose on the object (centre, surface normal for a suction cup), express it in the robot base with the hand-eye transform, check it with the inverse kinematics, and send it to MoveIt.

Libraries: OpenCV (images, calibration, markers), Open3D and PCL (point clouds), and in ROS 2 image_pipeline, aruco_ros and easy_handeye2.

The essentials
  • Intrinsic calibration (checkerboard) gives K: focal length and image centre, plus distortion.
  • A marker gives its pose by PnP; hand-eye calibration (A X = X B) links the camera to the robot.

7

Reinforcement learning for robots

18 min
You will learn

the vocabulary of reinforcement learning, the Q-learning update and the policy-gradient idea, how rewards are designed, and how a policy learned in simulation is moved to a real robot.

7.1 The loop: agent and environment

At each step the robot (the agent) sees a state s, chooses an action a with its policy π(a | s), and receives a reward r and a new state. Its goal: maximise the sum of the future rewards, each discounted by γ per step (0 < γ < 1):

G = r0 + γ r1 + γ² r2 + …

No one tells the robot what the right action is: it learns from the consequences, by trial and error. This is the difference with supervised learning (perception course, lesson 8).

7.2 Values and Q-learning

Q(s, a) is the expected return if the robot takes action a in state s and then acts well. If Q is known, the best action is simply the one with the largest Q. Q-learning improves its estimate after each step:

Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α [ r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a) ]

Example: Q = 2, reward 1, best value of the next state 5, γ = 0.9, learning rate α = 0.1: the target is 1 + 0.9 × 5 = 5.5, the error 3.5, and Q becomes 2 + 0.1 × 3.5 = 2.35. Repeated millions of times, Q converges. With a neural network instead of a table, this is DQN.

7.3 Continuous actions: policy gradient

Robot joints need continuous actions (torques, target angles), where “max over all actions” is impractical. Policy-gradient methods train a neural network that outputs the action directly, and nudge its weights so that actions followed by high returns become more likely. PPO (the most used in robotics) and SAC are the standard algorithms.

7.4 Rewards: say what you want, not how

TaskReward terms
walk forward+ speed towards the target, − energy (Σ τ²), − falls, − jerky joints
reach a point− distance of the tool to the target, + bonus when reached, − collisions
Reward hacking

The agent maximises exactly what you wrote, not what you meant. Rewarding “speed of the body” only, a simulated robot may learn to throw itself forward or to vibrate in a strange way. Watch the videos of the learned behaviour, and add penalty terms.

7.5 From simulation to the real robot

Learning needs millions of trials: on a real robot that would take years and break it. Policies are trained in simulation (Isaac Lab, MuJoCo), thousands of robots in parallel on a GPU, in hours. The sim-to-real gap (friction, masses, delays, sensor noise differ) is closed by:

  • Domain randomisation: change masses, friction, delays and noise randomly during training, so that the real robot is just one more variation.
  • Good actuator models: learn or measure the real response of the motors and put it in the simulator.
  • Safety layers: limits on torques and speeds, and a classical controller ready to take over.

This recipe is behind the agile quadrupeds and humanoids of recent years. For manipulation, imitation learning from human demonstrations, and foundation models, are often combined with it (perception course, lesson 8).

Key terms

  • agent / environment
  • policy
  • reward
  • Q-learning
  • policy gradient (PPO)
  • reward hacking
  • sim-to-real
  • domain randomisation
The essentials
  • Agent, state, action, reward, policy; goal: maximise G = Σ γᵏ rₖ.
  • Q-learning: Q ← Q + α (r + γ max Q′ − Q). Continuous actions: policy gradient (PPO, SAC).

Grasping in practice: choose, size and test a gripper

24 min
You will learn

to choose a gripper from the part and the cycle, to compute the gripping force with the accelerations, to size a pneumatic gripper and a vacuum system, to check the robot payload and wrist moment, to know when a grasp holds (friction cones), and to test it like an engineer.

In industry the gripper is often the part of the cell that fails first, and the one that is chosen last. This lesson follows the order an engineer uses: the part → the type of gripper → the force → the robot → the tests. It continues lesson 3 of “Robots in the real world”, which presents the families of end effectors.

8.1 Start from the part and the cycle

Before looking at a catalogue, write down the facts. Each line of this list removes some grippers:

8.2 The gripping force, with the accelerations

With a friction grip (force-fit), the jaws hold the part only by friction. With n jaws pressing with a normal force N each, the friction must carry the weight and the inertia of the part during the fastest move, with a safety factor S (2 is usual):

n · μ · N ≥ S · m · ( g + a )    ⇒    N ≥ S · m · ( g + a ) / ( n · μ )

Here a is the largest acceleration of the robot that pulls the part along the fingers (an up move of the robot, or the braking of a down move). If the part is held in a V-shaped jaw of angle α, the wedge multiplies the friction: μeff = μ / sin(α/2), that is 1.41 × μ for a 90° V. With a form-fit grip (fingers that go under a shoulder or into a groove), the shape carries the load and the force only keeps the part in place.

Worked example: a 2 kg steel part

m = 2 kg, a = 5 m/s², S = 2, two jaws, so S · m · (g + a) = 2 × 2 × 14.81 = 59.2 N to carry by friction.

  • Steel fingers on steel (μ = 0.15): N = 59.2 / (2 × 0.15) ≈ 197 N per jaw.
  • The same with 90° V-jaws (μeff = 0.21): 140 N.
  • Rubber pads (μ = 0.8): 37 N, a gripper five times smaller.

8.3 Pneumatic or electric gripper

A pneumatic gripper is a piston: its force is the pressure times the piston area, reduced by the efficiency of the mechanism. At 6 bar with a 25 mm piston: A = π × 0.0125² = 4.9·10⁻⁴ m², F = 6·10⁵ × 4.9·10⁻⁴ ≈ 295 N, about 118 N per jaw after a mechanism efficiency of 0.8. Plan the force at the lowest pressure of the factory network (often 5 bar, not 6).

Fpiston = p · π D² / 4    Njaw ≈ η · Fpiston / n

8.4 Sizing a vacuum system

A cup holds with the pressure difference times its area. The safety factor depends on how the load pulls on the cup: along its axis (cup on the top face of a box) S = 2 is usual; across it (cup on a vertical face), only friction holds and the force is divided by μ (about 0.5), so S = 4 or more.

Fcup = ΔP · π d² / 4    number of cups ≥ S · m · ( g + a ) / Fcup

Worked example: a 6 kg box

Cups on the top face, a = 5 m/s², S = 2: the cups must give 2 × 6 × 14.81 ≈ 178 N. One 40 mm cup at ΔP = 60 kPa gives 60 000 × π × 0.02² ≈ 75 N, so 178 / 75 = 2.4 → 3 cups, and 4 in practice to keep the box level and to tolerate one leaking cup. Cardboard is porous: choose the vacuum generator by its flow, and check the real vacuum with the real boxes.

  • Vacuum generator: a venturi ejector (compressed air, small, fast, but it uses air all the time) or an electric pump (efficient, for big flows or no air).
  • Cup: flat for smooth flat parts (precise), bellows for uneven or slightly inclined surfaces; silicone for food and heat, NBR for oily parts.
  • Sensing and release: a vacuum switch confirms the grasp before the robot moves; a short blow of air releases the part at once.

8.5 Check the robot: payload and wrist moment

The robot carries the gripper and the part. The payload of the data sheet is a mass at a given distance from the flange: the load diagram of the robot gives the allowed mass for each offset of the centre of mass. Compute the total mass and its centre of mass along the tool axis:

mt = mg + mp    d = ( mg dg + mp dp ) / mt    Mwrist = mt · g · d

Example: a 1.2 kg gripper with its centre of mass at 0.06 m and a 2 kg part at 0.15 m: mt = 3.2 kg, d = (0.072 + 0.30) / 3.2 ≈ 0.116 m, and the static moment on the wrist is 3.2 × 9.81 × 0.116 ≈ 3.6 N·m. A 5 kg robot may accept 5 kg at 0.05 m but only about 3 kg at 0.12 m: always read the load diagram, and enter the real tool data in the controller (mass, centre of mass, inertia), otherwise the dynamics and the collision detection are wrong.

8.6 When does a grasp hold? Friction cones

At a contact, the force can lean away from the normal by at most the angle arctan μ without slipping: the possible forces form a friction cone. For two fingers (an antipodal grasp), the part can be squeezed and held in any direction if the line between the two contacts lies inside both cones. In practice: the angle between each contact normal and the line joining the contacts must be smaller than arctan μ.

With μ = 0.5 the cone is 26.6° wide on each side, so a part with sides tilted by 12° is held; with μ = 0.15 (steel on steel, 8.5°) the same part is squeezed out of the fingers like a wet soap. This is why tapered or round parts need shaped fingers. With more fingers, the general tool is the grasp matrix G, which maps the contact forces to the wrench on the part: a grasp has force closure if, with forces inside the cones, G can produce any wrench.

8.7 Test the grasp like an engineer

  1. Check the grasp before moving: jaw position in the expected window (electric gripper), end-of-stroke sensor, or vacuum switch.
  2. Shake test: run the real cycle at 100 % speed and acceleration with the lightest and the heaviest parts, then with oily or dusty parts.
  3. Loss of energy: cut the air or the power during a move. A spring, a self-locking gear or a check valve must keep the part.
  4. Endurance: thousands of cycles; check wear of pads and cups, and the drift of the part position in the fingers.
