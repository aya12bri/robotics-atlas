Definition

A quadruped robot walks on four legs, like a dog. Each leg has three joints (sideways at the hip, forward at the hip, the knee), so 12 motors in all. On four legs it is stable standing still and it goes where wheels cannot: stairs, gravel, mud. Examples: Boston Dynamics Spot, ANYbotics ANYmal, Unitree Go2.

Gait and body

Press Trot to make it walk; the speed slider sets the step length and the rhythm. The other sliders raise the body and tilt it forward or sideways: the feet stay on the ground.

Swings the whole leg forward and back. With the knee, it moves the foot in the plane of the leg. Its motor sits in the body, close to the hip.

Bends the lower leg. On most quadrupeds the knee points backwards. Its motor is not in the knee but in the hip, and a rod pulls the knee (seen in X-ray): the leg stays light and fast.

A rubber ball that grips the ground. The robot feels when a foot touches the ground from the motor currents, or with a contact sensor.

Stereo depth cameras at the front, the back and on both sides: the robot sees the steps and the obstacles in every direction.

The battery fills the middle of the body and gives about 90 minutes of walking. The computer runs the balance loop and the vision. Seen in X-ray, with the IMU.

The abduction joint opens the leg sideways. It keeps the feet under the body when the body rolls, and lets the robot step sideways.

Kinematics: four legs of three joints

Each leg is a small serial arm with 3 joints: abduction (the leg opens sideways), hip and knee. With the 6 degrees of freedom of the body, the robot has 18 coordinates to control, and 12 motors to do it. The feet on the ground close the loop: like a parallel robot, the body can move in all 6 directions while the feet stay still.

Inverse kinematics of one leg

First the abduction turns the plane of the leg so that it contains the foot; then, in that plane, hip and knee work like a two-link arm. On most quadrupeds the knee points backwards, so the second solution of the elbow is used.

Abduction: γ = atan2(z, −y)   Distance in the plane: d = √(x² + y² + z²)   Knee: cos(π − θ_k) = (L1² + L2² − d²) / (2 L1 L2)

Gaits

A gait says which legs move together. The duty factor is the part of the cycle each foot spends on the ground: above 0.5 at least two feet always touch the ground.

What is inside each joint?

  • 12 actuators: three per leg (abduction, hip, knee), each a brushless motor with a planetary gearbox, all placed in the body or at the hip so that the legs stay light.
  • Balance: an IMU in the body, an encoder on every joint and the feeling of the feet; on 4 legs the robot is stable standing still, and when it trots two feet are always on the ground.
  • Uses: inspection of factories, tunnels, oil rigs and power plants, carrying sensors where wheels cannot go (stairs, gravel, mud).

The quadruped really trots: the legs go in two diagonal pairs (front left with rear right, then front right with rear left). For each leg, a small inverse kinematics finds the three angles (abduction, hip, knee) that put the foot where the gait wants it, even when you tilt the body. The model is simplified; real robots such as Spot or ANYmal add force control in every joint.

Where are quadrupeds used?

Robot dogs carry sensors where wheels cannot go: stairs, gravel, mud, cables on the floor. They are mostly used for inspection, often driven from far away or following a recorded route on their own.

  • Weaknesses: about 90 minutes of battery, slower and more fragile than a robot on wheels or tracks on flat ground.
