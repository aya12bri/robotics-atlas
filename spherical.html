Spherical R-R-P

← Robot list · Learn the basics

The base turns (q1), the arm tilts up and down (q2), and a telescopic arm slides in and out (q3). The tool moves in spherical coordinates: two angles and a distance, like a gun turret or a camera on a tripod with a zoom. This is the structure of the Unimate, the very first industrial robot (1961), and of the Stanford arm.

Joints

Two rotations, then one slide. Move a slider, or type a value in a number box.

Kinematic diagram (schéma cinématique)

Frames:

The circles and bars are the revolute joints q1 (vertical axis) and q2 (horizontal axis), the square is the sliding joint q3. The side view turns with q1, so the arm always lies in the picture. Axes: x red, y green, z blue. ⊙ points at you, ⊗ points away.

DH table (standard)

Frame i is attached to the axis of joint i+1. Each row is: Ai = Rz(θ) · Tz(d) · Tx(a) · Rx(α). The +90° on θ2 lines the axis x up so that z2, the direction of the slide, follows the arm. Orange numbers are the current values.

DHM table (modified DH)

Frame i is attached to the axis of joint i. Each row is: Ai = Rx(αi−1) · Tx(ai−1) · Rz(θ) · Tz(d).

Result: where is the tool?


    
The last column is the position of the tool in the base frame {0}. Both tables give exactly this same matrix.


    
The models in three lines

    
Direct model (MGD):    x = q3 · cos q2 · cos q1    y = q3 · cos q2 · sin q1    z = h + q3 · sin q2
Inverse model (MGI):   q1 = atan2(y, x)    q2 = atan2(z − h, √(x² + y²))    q3 = √(x² + y² + (z − h)²)      (h = 0.40 m)

    
The joints are the spherical coordinates of the tool seen from the shoulder: azimuth q1, elevation q2 and distance q3. Like the cylindrical robot, the inverse model has a single answer except on the vertical line above the shoulder, where q1 is undefined (singularity).


    
Workspace

    
    
The tool stays between two spheres centred on the shoulder (radius q3 min and q3 max), between the lowest and highest tilt of q2: a thick spherical shell cut by two cones. The volume grows with the cube of the reach: a long telescopic arm covers a huge space, but every millimetre of play at the shoulder becomes a large error at the tool.

Where is a spherical robot used?

      
Historically: spot welding and die casting (Unimate at General Motors, 1961), forging, loading presses and machine tools. Today the structure survives in heavy hydraulic manipulators, in some teaching robots, and in the positioning of telescopes, antennas, cameras and radiotherapy arms (two angles and a distance).

      
Strengths: a large reach and workspace for a compact body, simple maths, a strong hydraulic arm is easy to build. Weaknesses: accuracy drops far from the shoulder (errors grow with the distance), the telescopic arm is heavy and hard to seal, and it cannot reach near its own base. The 6-axis articulated robot has replaced it in most factories.

      
End effector (tool)

      
Welding guns, grippers for hot parts, and, with a wrist of 2 or 3 axes, any tool that must also be oriented.
