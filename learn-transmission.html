1

Transmission and transformation: why robots need them

15 min
You will learn

the difference between transmitting and transforming a motion, the eight reasons for them in a robot, the parts of an actuation chain, the kinds of actuators, and the difference between an actuator and an end effector.

In one sentence

Motors spin fast with little torque; joints need to turn slowly with a lot of torque, sometimes in a straight line or around another axis: transmissions adapt the motion, transformations change its nature.

1.1 Two words

TransmissionTransformation
What it doescarries the mechanical power from the motor to the joint, adapting speed and torquechanges the nature of the motion: type, direction, amplitude
Examplesgear reducers, harmonic drives, belts, chainsscrew-nut and rack-pinion (rotation → translation), cam (rotation → translation), bevel and worm gears (another axis), levers (amplitude)

1.2 Eight reasons

  1. Adapt power: the motor turns fast with little torque; the joint needs the opposite.
  2. Compactness: a small, light motor still produces enough torque.
  3. Precision: dividing the speed divides the position error of the motor by the same ratio.
  4. Carry energy to joints far from the motor (belts, cables in light arms).
  5. Convert the type of motion: rotary motor, linear joint.
  6. Change the direction or the axis (bevel, worm gears).
  7. Adapt the amplitude (levers, linkages: a small rotation opens a gripper wide).
  8. In end effectors: share one motor between several fingers (tendons, gears).

1.3 The actuation chain

ActuatorStrengthsWeaknesses
Electric (servo, stepper)precise, clean, easy to controllimited torque for heavy tasks → reducer
Hydraulic (cylinders, motors)very high force and torquebulky, leaks, maintenance of the fluid
Pneumatic (air cylinders)simple, fast, cheapair is compressible: hard to stop in the middle

The actuator is the muscle: it turns energy into motion at a joint. The end effector (gripper, suction cup, welding torch, screwdriver, hand) is the tool at the end of the arm that does the work on the object. Actuators give motion and force; the end effector applies them to the task.

Worked example

A motor gives 0.5 N·m at 3000 rpm. A reducer of ratio 100 with an efficiency of 85 %: output speed 3000 / 100 = 30 rpm, output torque 0.5 × 100 × 0.85 = 42.5 N·m. The power is (almost) the same: 157 W in, 133 W out.

Key terms

  • mechanical transmission
  • motion transformation
  • reducer, gear ratio
  • actuation chain
  • actuator
  • end effector
  • efficiency η
The essentials
  • Transmission adapts speed and torque; transformation changes the type, direction or amplitude of the motion.
  • Chain: power supply → amplifier → servomotor → transmission → joint. ω ÷ N, T × N × η.
  • Actuators (electric, hydraulic, pneumatic) move; the end effector does the work.

Belts, chains, friction wheels, shafts and cams

You will learn

how each common drive transmits motion, the rule of the speed ratio, which ones reverse the direction, and their strengths, weaknesses and uses in robots.

In one sentence

When nothing slips, the speed at the rim is the same on both wheels: v = ω₁ r₁ = ω₂ r₂, so a small wheel driving a big one slows down and gains torque.

i = n₁ / n₂ = d₂ / d₁    T₂ = T₁ × i × η

Gears: types, vocabulary and ratios

25 min
You will learn

the main types of gears, the words of gears (pitch circle, module, pressure angle…), the formulas of speed, torque, power and efficiency, the direction of rotation, idler gears and compound trains.

In one sentence

Two gears are two wheels whose teeth cannot slip: their pitch circles roll on each other, so the speed ratio is the ratio of their numbers of teeth.

TypeShaftsFeature
Spurparallelstraight teeth, simple, the most common
Helicalparallelangled teeth: smoother and quieter, higher loads, but axial forces
Bevelintersecting (often 90°)changes the axis
Worm and wheelcrossed at 90°very high reduction, often self-locking
Planetarycoaxialsun, planets, ring: compact, high torque density
Cycloidal, harmonic, magneticcoaxialspecial types for precision robots

3.1 The vocabulary

  • Pitch circle: the circle that defines the “size” of the gear; the pitch circles of two gears are tangent. Its diameter is the pitch diameter d.
  • Circular pitch p: the arc between two teeth on the pitch circle. Module m = p / π (in mm): a round number, the same for two gears that mesh. Then d = m Z (Z teeth).
  • Diametral pitch (inch system): teeth per inch of diameter, the inverse of the module. Pressure angle: the angle of the force on the tooth (14.5°, 20° or 25°); it must be the same on both gears.
  • Driving gear (gets the motor power), driven gear, idler gear (between them, keeps the direction), gear train, gearbox (closed gear train).

3.2 Speed, torque, power, efficiency

GR = NB / NA = ωA / ωB = RB / RA    η = PB / PA = TB ωB / (TA ωA)    TB = η GR TA

Where does the torque formula come from? At the contact, the tooth of A pushes the tooth of B with a tangential force Ft = TA / RA; this same force acts on B at the radius RB: TB = Ft RB = TA RB / RA (losses aside). In a spur gear the real force is inclined by the pressure angle φ: F = Ft / cos φ, with a radial part Ft tan φ that pushes the shafts apart. A helical gear adds an axial part Ft tan ψ (ψ the helix angle).

3.3 Direction and gear trains

Each external mesh reverses the direction: with an odd number of gears in a row the output turns like the input, with an even number it turns the other way. An idler gear changes the direction but not the ratio. In a compound train, two gears share a shaft and the ratios multiply:

Worked example: a simple gear train

Input A 12 teeth at 1800 rpm. B 36 and C 15 on the same shaft, C drives D 45. Ratio (36/12)(45/15) = 9; output speed 1800 / 9 = 200 rpm; two external meshes → the output turns in the same direction as the input. With 98 % per mesh, torque × 9 × 0.98² = × 8.6.

Key terms

  • spur, helical, bevel gear
  • pitch circle, pitch diameter
  • module m
  • pressure angle
  • gear ratio GR
  • idler gear
  • compound gear train
The essentials
  • d = m Z, centre distance a = m (Z₁ + Z₂) / 2; same module and pressure angle to mesh.
  • GR = N_B / N_A = ω_A / ω_B, T_B = η GR T_A; each external mesh reverses the direction.
  • Compound train: the ratios multiply; an idler only changes the direction.

Backlash, choice and maintenance of gears

15 min
You will learn

what backlash is, where it comes from, why it hurts robots and how to avoid it; how to choose a gearbox, and how to maintain it.

In one sentence

Backlash is the small gap between teeth: when the motor reverses, it turns a little before the output moves, and this lost motion becomes a position error at the end of the arm.

4.1 Backlash and lost motion

Backlash is the clearance between mating teeth, measured as the angle the input can turn before the other flank of the tooth is touched. It comes from manufacturing tolerances, thermal expansion and wear, the room needed for the lubricant, and the profile of the teeth. Lost motion is wider: backlash + elastic deformation + hysteresis.

  • Effects: lower positioning accuracy, noise, vibrations, shocks at each reversal; critical in robots, CNC and motion control.
  • Remedies: preloaded or anti-backlash gears (two half-gears pushed apart by springs), high-precision manufacturing, and low-backlash reducers: planetary (a few arcminutes), cycloidal, harmonic drives (almost zero).
Worked example

A backlash of 10 arcmin at the joint (1/6 of a degree) and an arm of 1 m: at the end of the arm the error is 1000 × (1/6) × π / 180 ≈ 2.9 mm at each reversal. A good industrial robot repeats to 0.02–0.05 mm: its reducers must have well under 1 arcmin.

4.2 Choosing a gearbox

CriterionQuestion to ask
Ratiohow many input turns per output turn? (choose motor and gearbox together)
Mountingbase, shaft, coaxial or at 90°?
Environmentnoise, space, dust, water, food contact, temperature
Efficiency and backlashpower application or precision? low backlash costs more
Maintenancetype of lubricant, inspection, replacement

4.3 Maintenance

  • Lubricate as specified (oil or grease) and replace worn parts.
  • Keep dust and water out; store dry and clean when unused.
  • Monitor noise, vibration and temperature: their rise announces a failure (predictive maintenance).
  • Avoid misalignment when reassembling.

Key terms

  • backlash
  • lost motion
  • arcminute
  • anti-backlash gear
  • preload
  • predictive maintenance
The essentials
  • Backlash = clearance between teeth; lost motion = backlash + deformation + hysteresis.
  • Error at the tip = arm length × backlash (rad): 10′ on 1 m ≈ 2.9 mm.
  • Remedies: preloaded gears, precision, planetary, cycloidal, harmonic drives. Choose ratio, mounting, environment, backlash, maintenance.

Changing the motion: worm, rack, bevel, screw and nut

20 min
You will learn

how a worm gear, a rack and pinion, a bevel gear and a screw-nut transform a rotation, their formulas, which ones are self-locking, and where robots use them.

In one sentence

A rotation can become a slower rotation around another axis (worm, bevel) or a straight motion (rack, screw); the price is friction, and sometimes the gain is that the load cannot drive the motor back.

MechanismFormula+−In robots
Worm and wheelGR = z₂ / z₁ (wheel teeth / worm starts)huge reduction in one stage, compact, self-locking (lifting safety)high friction (low efficiency), wear, heat, lubricationgrippers, lifting axes, pan-tilt units
Rack and piniond = m Z, p = π m, v = ω d / 2simple, fast, long strokeslimited load, backlash, not self-locking, stroke = rack lengthgantry robots (long axes), steering, grippers
Bevel gearsGR = Z₂ / Z₁right angle, high torque; spiral: quieter; hypoid: offset axesalignment, noise, costwrists of industrial robots, differentials
Screw and nutv = n × lead; F = 2π η T / leadhuge force, precise; ball screw: η ≈ 0.9speed limited by the length (whip); trapezoidal screws wearlinear axes, SCARA quill, electric grippers

Self-locking: a worm with one start has a small lead angle λ; when λ is smaller than the friction angle (tan φ = μ), the wheel cannot drive the worm: the load stays in place without a brake. The same holds for a trapezoidal screw with a small lead, never for a ball screw.

Worked examples
  • Rack and pinion: Z = 20, m = 2 mm → d = 40 mm. At 60 rpm (2π rad/s) the rack moves at v = 2π × 20 mm = 126 mm/s.
  • Ball screw, lead 5 mm, η = 0.9, motor torque 1 N·m: F = 2π × 0.9 × 1 / 0.005 ≈ 1130 N.
  • Worm with 1 start, wheel with 40 teeth: ratio 40 in one stage; at 1200 rpm the wheel turns at 30 rpm.

Key terms

  • worm gear
  • self-locking
  • rack and pinion
  • bevel gear, hypoid
  • lead screw, ball screw
  • lead angle
The essentials
  • Worm: GR = z₂ / z₁, often self-locking; rack: v = ω m Z / 2; screw: v = n × lead, F = 2π η T / lead.
  • Bevel gears change the axis (often 90°): robot wrists, differentials.
  • Self-locking: lead angle smaller than the friction angle; a ball screw never is.

Planetary gears, harmonic drives and the reducers of industrial robots

25 min
You will learn

how a planetary gear works and its three ratios, how a harmonic drive reaches 100:1 with almost no backlash, the cycloidal reducer, and which reducer sits in which axis of an industrial robot.

In one sentence

Planetary gears share the load between several planets in a compact ring; harmonic drives bend a flexible gear so that a difference of two teeth gives a reduction of 50 to 160 with no backlash: the two favourite reducers of robots.

6.1 The planetary (epicyclic) gear

A sun gear in the centre, planet gears around it held by a carrier, and a ring with internal teeth. Fix one member, drive another, the third is the output. Its strengths: high power density, several possible ratios, high torque, load shared between the planets. Willis’ formula links the three speeds: (ωs − ωc) / (ωr − ωc) = −Nr / Ns.

6.2 The harmonic drive (strain wave gear)

  • Wave generator: an elliptical cam with a thin bearing, on the input.
  • Flexspline: a thin, flexible cup with external teeth, deformed into an ellipse; it is the output.
  • Circular spline: a rigid ring with internal teeth, 2 teeth more than the flexspline, fixed.

GR = Ne / (Nc − Ne)    (Nc = 202, Ne = 200 → GR = 100, output reversed)

The teeth mesh at the two ends of the ellipse at once, so many teeth share the load: high torque for its size, and the preload of the flexible teeth leaves almost no backlash. More expensive, and the flexspline has a limited life under shocks. The cycloidal reducer (RV reducer) reaches the same precision with a lobed disc rolling in a ring of pins; it is stiffer and stands shocks, hence its place in the heavy axes.

6.3 In an industrial robot

AxisTypical reducerWhy
Axes 1 to 3 (base, shoulder, elbow) of a large robotcycloidal (RV) reducerhigh torque, stiffness, shocks
Wrist axes 4 to 6, cobots, small robotsharmonic drivelight, compact, zero backlash, hollow shaft for the cables
Axes 5 and 6 (often)motor in the forearm + timing belt or bevel gears + reducerkeep the wrist small and light (each maker — ABB, KUKA, Yaskawa, FANUC — has its own layout)
Hobby servomotorsspur gear train in plastic or metalcheap; some backlash

Key terms

  • planetary gear: sun, planets, carrier, ring
  • Willis formula
  • harmonic drive: wave generator, flexspline, circular spline
  • cycloidal (RV) reducer
  • torque density
The essentials
  • Planetary: fixed ring 1 + N_r/N_s; fixed sun 1 + N_s/N_r; fixed carrier −N_r/N_s; N_r = N_s + 2N_p.
  • Harmonic drive: GR = N_e / (N_c − N_e), output reversed, almost no backlash.
  • Robots: cycloidal (RV) in the heavy axes, harmonic in wrists and cobots, belts or bevels to bring motors back.

Sizing the drive of a robot joint

25 min
You will learn

how to check that a motor and a reducer can move a joint: torque and speed at the motor, reflected inertia, the ratio that minimises the acceleration torque, efficiency, and a method in five steps.

In one sentence

Through a reducer of ratio N, the motor turns N times faster, needs N times less torque, and sees the inertia of the load divided by N²: a good ratio balances the motor speed against its torque.

ωm = N ωL    Tm = Jm N αL + (JL αL + Tload) / (N η)    Jreflected = JL / N²

The first term is the torque to accelerate the rotor of the motor (it grows with N), the second the torque the load asks, brought back through the reducer (it falls with N). Without gravity, the acceleration torque is smallest for N* = √(JL / Jm): the reflected inertia then equals the motor inertia (“inertia matching”). In practice we accept a load-to-motor inertia ratio up to about 5 to 10 for a responsive servo.

7.1 A method in five steps

  1. Write the motion of the joint: maximal speed ωL, acceleration αL, duty cycle.
  2. Compute the load: inertia JL of the arm and payload about the joint, gravity torque m g d, friction.
  3. Choose N so that N ωL stays under the maximal motor speed.
  4. Check the peak torque Tm against the peak torque of the motor, and the RMS torque over the cycle against its continuous torque (heating).
  5. Check the reducer: output torque, input speed, backlash, life; add a margin (1.5 to 2).
Worked example

JL = 2 kg·m², gravity torque 30 N·m, α = 10 rad/s², 180 °/s; motor Jm = 3·10⁻⁴ kg·m², 3 N·m peak, 3000 rpm; η = 0.85. With N = 15: Tm = 3·10⁻⁴ × 15 × 10 + (20 + 30) / (15 × 0.85) ≈ 3.97 N·m: too much. With N = 50: 0.15 + 50 / 42.5 ≈ 1.33 N·m and 30 rpm × 50 = 1500 rpm: both are fine. N* = √(2 / 3·10⁻⁴) ≈ 82 would minimise the acceleration part only.

Key terms

  • reflected inertia
  • inertia matching
  • peak torque, RMS torque
  • duty cycle
  • safety margin
The essentials
  • ω_m = N ω_L; T_m = J_m N α + (J_L α + T_load) / (N η); J reflected = J_L / N².
  • Inertia matching: N* = √(J_L / J_m); accept a load/motor inertia ratio of 5–10.
  • Check: peak torque, RMS torque (heating), max speed, reducer limits, margin 1.5–2.

    4 / 100² = 4·10⁻⁴ kg·m².

  2. J_L = 1 kg·m², J_m = 1·10⁻⁴ kg·m². Ratio of inertia matching?

    √(10⁴) = 100.

  3. Why is the RMS torque checked as well as the peak torque?

    The RMS torque sets the heating of the motor over the whole cycle.

8

Robot grippers: kinds, principles and design

25 min
You will learn

what a gripper does, the families of grippers (mechanical, vacuum, magnetic, soft, adhesive), how they hold a part (form-fit, force-fit, external, internal), the gripper frame, how the jaw force comes from the motor torque, and a complete design: a gripper for cups.

In one sentence

A gripper turns the motion of a motor into a temporary, well-placed contact with the part: it must hold the weight and the accelerations without dropping or damaging it.

8.1 What a gripper does

  • Keeps a definite position and orientation of the part relative to the robot.
  • Holds static (weight), dynamic (accelerations) and process forces and moments.
  • Sometimes does more: presses, fits, joins. That is why the wider word end effector is used. Most grippers come from tooling makers, not from the robot maker, or are designed for one task.
Family (principle)HowFor
Impactive: mechanical fingerstwo or more fingers squeeze the partrigid parts, precise placing
Astrictive: vacuum, magnetic, electroadhesiona vacuum under a cup, a magnetic field, an electric fieldflat surfaces, boxes, sheets; steel parts
Contigutive: adhesivesurface adhesion (chemical, thermal)light, thin, soft parts

Actuation: pneumatic (fast, cheap, two positions), electric (force and position controlled), hydraulic (very strong), servo-electric (programmable). Foam vacuum grippers use a high air flow and a moderate vacuum, the opposite of suction cups: they tolerate leaks, porous and uneven products without any setting.

8.2 How the part is held

  • Force-fit (by friction): the jaws press; friction μ N holds the part. Form-fit: the shape of the jaws surrounds the part; it holds even without friction (safer).
  • External, internal (in a bore) or both (tubes); radial or axial grip. Avoid point contacts: V-jaws or soft pads spread the force and centre the part.
  • Gripper frame: origin at the centre of the gripper; Ze along the approach direction; Ye in the plane of the finger motion; Xe completes a right-handed frame. It is the tool frame (TCP) given to the robot.

8.3 From motor torque to gripping force

screw: F = 2π η T / lead   rack (one pinion, two racks): F = η T / r per jaw   friction grip: n μ F ≥ S m (g + a)

n is the number of contact faces (2 for two jaws), S a safety factor (about 2), a the largest acceleration of the robot. In the BarrettHand, four motors with position sensors drive seven joints through worm gears and Bowden cables: each finger has one motor for its two joints, and the worm keeps the grasp when the power is off.

The design project: a gripper for tea and coffee cups

Cups of 50 to 500 g, 5 to 15 cm high, 4 to 10 cm in diameter, ceramic or plastic. A good answer: a parallel two-finger electric gripper (one motor, screw or rack, stroke ≥ 60 mm for 4 to 10 cm), with V-shaped soft silicone pads (μ ≈ 0.8, centres the cup, does not mark it), gripping the body below the rim, the handle free. Force: n μ F ≥ S m (g + a) with 0.5 kg, a = 5 m/s², S = 2, μ = 0.8, n = 2 → F ≥ 9.3 N; keep it under 30 N so as not to crush a thin plastic cup (force control). Motor: a lead-screw of 2 mm with η = 0.4 gives F = 2π × 0.4 × T / 0.002 → T ≈ 0.75 N·cm for 9.3 N: a small DC motor with a gearbox does it, and a self-locking screw keeps the grip if the power is cut.
