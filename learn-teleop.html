1

Teleoperation: acting at a distance

15 min
You will learn

what teleoperation is, where it is used, its history, the chain operator – master – channel – slave – environment, unilateral and bilateral teleoperation, and why perfect transparency is impossible.

1.1 More than a remote control

Word by word, teleoperation is action at a distance. A better definition: teleoperation extends the senses and the movements of a human to a remote environment. The operator decides and moves; a robot far away (or very small, or in a dangerous place) does the work; the operator sees, and sometimes feels, what the robot touches.

FieldWhy a human at a distance?Example
Nuclearradiation: nobody may go inhandling in hot cells, dismantling of reactors
Space (orbit, planets)the place is out of reachrobot arm of the space station, planetary rovers
Explorationdeep sea, volcanoes, ruins after an earthquakeunderwater robots (ROV) linked by a cable
Medicineprecision, small scale, or an expert who is far awaysurgical robots, tele-echography

1.2 A short history

  • 1920s: radioactive products are handled behind a wall with simple tongs.
  • 1947: the first mechanical master-slave arms: cables and gears link the arm held by the operator to the one in the hot cell, so the operator naturally feels the forces.
  • 1954: the first electrical master-slave: the two sides are linked by wires, so the distance and the uses grow.
  • 1980s: computer-assisted teleoperation: a computer between the two sides filters, scales, guides and adds autonomy.

1.3 The chain of a teleoperation

  • The operator (a human) gives the intention.
  • The master is the device the operator holds: a joystick, or a haptic interface, a small robot that can push back on the hand.
  • The communication channel carries the data: it adds delays, noise and losses.
  • The slave is the remote robot. It acts on the remote environment.

1.4 Unilateral or bilateral

In a unilateral teleoperation, orders only go from the master to the slave; the operator gets back an image at best. In a bilateral teleoperation, information also comes back to the master: the operator feels the contact forces (force feedback, or haptic feedback). Feeling a contact is essential to insert a part, to push without breaking, to cut tissue gently.

1.5 Transparency: the ideal that is never reached

A teleoperated system is transparent if the force, the speed and the position are transmitted faithfully between the remote environment and the operator: the operator would feel exactly as if their hand touched the remote object. In reality it is impossible: the master and the slave have their own mass and friction, the sensors are imperfect, and the channel adds delay. All the architectures of the next lessons are compromises between transparency and stability.

LinkTypical one-way delay
Cable in the same roomless than 1 ms
Wi-Fi5 to 50 ms
Internet across a continent30 to 150 ms
Geostationary satelliteabout 250 ms
Earth – Moon1.3 s
Earth – Mars3 to 22 minutes
Remember

The longer the delay, the less the operator can drive “live”: with minutes of delay (Mars), the rover receives goals and drives itself. This trade between teleoperation and autonomy is the subject of lesson 10.

Impedance: the master seen as a mass-spring-damper

20 min
You will learn

what an impedance is, the equation F = m ẍ + b ẋ + k x, what each term changes, how to read it in the Laplace domain, and a reminder of position, speed and force control.

2.1 Why an impedance?

In teleoperation the master, the slave and the environment exchange forces and motions. To describe how each one reacts, we use its impedance: the link between the force applied to it and the motion it gets. A light, free part has a small impedance (it moves easily); a wall has a huge one (it hardly moves).

F(t) = m ẍ(t) + b ẋ(t) + k x(t)

TermPhysical meaningWhat you feel when you push
m (kg): massinertiaresists changes of speed: hard to start and to stop
b (N·s/m): dampingviscosityresists the speed, like a spoon in honey
k (N/m): stiffnesselasticitypulls back towards the rest position, like a spring

2.2 In the Laplace domain

In mechanics the impedance is the ratio force / speed: F(s) = Z(s) V(s). As V(s) = s X(s), we also write F(s) = Zd(s) X(s), and for the mass-spring-damper:

Zd(s) = m s² + b s + k    ω0 = √(k/m)    ζ = b / (2√(k m))

Worked example: the master of TD1

m = 5 kg, b = 5 N·s/m, k = 0, pushed by F = 10 sin(t) N, so ω = 1 rad/s. Replace s by jω: Zd(j) = −m + j b = −5 + 5j, of modulus √50 ≈ 7.07 N/m. The position swings with an amplitude 10 / 7.07 ≈ 1.41 m around a mean that drifts: with no spring, nothing brings the mass back.

2.3 Reminder: three ways to command a robot

CommandWhat we imposeModel used
in positionthe joint positions Q or the pose X = (x, y, z, Rx, Ry, Rz)geometric models MGD / MGI
in speedQ̇ or Ẋ: a trajectory in the operational spacekinematic models MCD / MCI (the Jacobian)
in forcethe forces and torques, through Q̈ and Ẍdynamic models MDD / MDI
What makes a good master

Light and with little friction (small m and b), so that it disappears in free space; and able to render a high stiffness k, so that a remote wall feels hard. These two wishes are the heart of haptic design.

  • ω₀ = √(k/m), ζ = b / (2√(km)); for a sine force of pulsation ω, the amplitude is A / |k − m ω² + j b ω|.

3

The position-position architecture (TD1)

20 min
You will learn

the definitions of master and slave, the forces on each one, how two virtual springs link them, the equations to build in Simulink, and what you observe when you change masses, damping and gains.

3.1 Master and slave, alone

The master is the system moved by the operator: it gives the set point. The slave is the system that reproduces the motion at a distance. Both are modelled as masses with damping (no spring: they stay where they are left).

AloneIn the teleoperation chain
Forces on the masteroperator force Fh, damping −dm ẋm+ the force sent back by the slave side, Fmaster
Forces on the slavedamping −ds ẋs (and a test force)+ the command Fslave computed from the master position (− the force of the environment, lesson 4)

3.2 Two virtual springs

Each side sends its position. Two correctors (two proportional controllers) act like springs: one pushes the slave towards the master, the other pulls the master towards the slave.

mm ẍm = Fh − dm ẋm + Kpm (xs − xm)    ms ẍs = −ds ẋs + Kps (xm − xs)

In Simulink: on the master side, add three forces (Fh, the damping, Fmaster), divide by mm to get the acceleration, integrate once for the speed and once more for the position (check the units: N / kg = m/s²). On the slave side, two forces (Fslave and the damping). Use a variable-step solver (or ode45) and one scope per quantity.

3.3 What you observe

  • The slave lags behind the master: its spring must first stretch to produce the force that moves it.
  • Larger gains K make the slave follow more closely (stiffer link), but too large gains make the system oscillate, all the more with delays and sampling.
  • A heavier slave lags more and overshoots more; more damping calms the oscillations but slows everything.
  • The operator feels the slave even in free space: the spring Kpm pulls the hand back whenever the slave lags. That is the lack of transparency of position-position.
Worked example: constant push

Master and slave of 5 kg, dm = ds = 5 N·s/m, K = 100 N/m. The operator pushes with a constant 10 N. In steady state both move at the same speed v; the only forces that stay are the two dampings: v = 10 / (5 + 5) = 1 m/s. The slave needs ds v = 5 N, given by its spring: the gap is xm − xs = 5 / 100 = 5 cm. Double K and the gap halves.

Position – position
✓ simple to build✓ good precision of the pose, no position drift
✗ little transparencythe operator feels the dynamics of the slave and the coupling spring, not only the environment

Key terms

  • position-position
  • virtual spring, coupling gain
  • steady state
  • position drift
  • Simulink, variable-step solver
The essentials
  • Position-position: F_slave = K_ps (x_m − x_s), F_master = K_pm (x_s − x_m): two virtual springs.
  • The slave lags; larger K → tighter following but more risk of oscillation.
  • Simple, precise, no drift, but little transparency: the operator feels the slave and the coupling.

Touching a remote wall (TD2)

20 min
You will learn

how to model a wall that the slave can touch, three sets of parameters (rigid, medium, soft), where the wall force enters the diagram, and why the operator feels a rigid wall as a soft one in position-position.

4.1 A model of the wall

The wall is a spring and a damper that act only when the slave enters it. It is infinitely heavy (it never moves). The damping of penetration dpen keeps the contact stable, as in a real material that dissipates energy.

Fenv = 0  if  xs ≤ xmur    Fenv = max(0, Kmur (xs − xmur) + dpen ẋs)  otherwise

WallKmur (N/m)dpen (N·s/m)Feels like
rigid10 000100wood, metal
medium1 00050hard rubber
soft10010foam, soft tissue

In Simulink, build a subsystem Environnement_Mur with the inputs xs and vs and the output Fenv; a Relational Operator compares xs with xmur and a Switch chooses between 0 and the formula (or write it in a MATLAB Function). Test it alone with a sine position: Fenv must be 0 before the wall and positive after. Then subtract Fenv from the forces of the slave.

4.2 Does the operator feel the wall?

Yes, but indirectly: when the slave is blocked, the master keeps moving, the gap grows, and the spring Kpm pushes the hand back. The operator feels the coupling spring in series with the wall. Two springs in series are softer than the softer one:

Kfelt = K · Kmur / (K + Kmur) < min(K, Kmur)

Worked example

K = 100 N/m and a rigid wall of 10 000 N/m: Kfelt = 106 / 10 100 ≈ 99 N/m. To feel 10 N, the master must go about 10 cm “into” the wall: a wall of steel feels like a soft cushion. To feel it harder, raise K: with 1 000 N/m, Kfelt ≈ 909 N/m and only 1.1 cm. But a stiff coupling oscillates sooner (lessons 5 and 6).

The lab uses the parameters of question 6 of TD2: master 0.2 kg and 1.5 N·s/m (a light haptic device), slave 5 kg and 5 N·s/m, wall at 5 cm. The operator is modelled as a force plus the stiffness and damping of a human arm, otherwise the light master would fly away. Look at three numbers: the force felt, the stiffness felt, and how far the master goes beyond the wall.

Key terms

  • remote environment
  • contact model
  • penetration damping
  • springs in series
  • stiffness felt
The essentials
  • Wall: F_env = max(0, K_mur (x_s − x_mur) + d_pen ẋ_s) inside the wall, 0 outside; it is subtracted from the slave forces.
  • Rigid 10 000 N/m, medium 1 000, soft 100 (with d_pen 100, 50, 10 N·s/m).
  • In position-position the operator feels K·K_mur/(K + K_mur): a rigid wall feels as soft as the coupling.

The bilateral architectures: force-position, force-force, four channels

25 min
You will learn

what each architecture sends in each direction, its strengths and weaknesses, why force feedback can destabilise, and how the imperfections of a force sensor (noise, resolution) change the feel.

5.1 Seven ways to link master and slave

An architecture is defined by what crosses the channel in each direction: positions, speeds, forces, or a mix. Read the name as “master side – slave side”: in force-position the master renders a force to the hand and the slave is commanded in position; in position-speed the position of the master sets the speed of the slave.

5.2 Force-position: better feel, but beware of the gain α

A force sensor on the slave measures Fenv; the master applies −α Fenv to the hand. With α = 1 the operator feels the real force. But the loop “hand → master → slave → wall → sensor → hand” has a gain: if it is larger than 1, a small push comes back bigger, then bigger again: the oscillation grows. A stiff environment and a light master make it worse.

SolutionNegative impact
lower the force feedback (α < 1)the force felt is smaller than the real one
add dampingthe system reacts more slowly
make the system passiveloss of transparency

5.3 An imperfect force sensor

  • Noise (band-limited white noise): the operator feels a vibration even in free space, and a noisy feedback can excite oscillations. Filtering helps but adds delay.
  • Resolution (quantisation): the force jumps by steps; a coarse step makes light contacts disappear and gives a “grainy” feel.
  • In Simulink: a Quantizer block and a Band-Limited White Noise block on the measured force.
What the lab shows
  • Force-position with α = 1 and the medium wall: the oscillations grow. Lower α to 0.5: it becomes stable, but you feel only half the force.
  • Force-force and 4 channels (Lawrence): the feel is good but the positions drift apart, because no position crosses the channel.
  • 4 channels position-force with high gains: the operator feels most of the wall stiffness, without drift: the best compromise here.

6

Delay, stability and wave variables

20 min
You will learn

why a delay can make a teleoperation unstable, the idea of passivity, the wave variables and their impedance b, their limits (drift, wave reflection), and the other answers to long delays.

6.1 Why a delay destabilises

With a delay T each way, the force felt by the operator describes the contact of 2T earlier. Imagine pushing a wall: the force comes back late, when the hand has already gone further, so it comes back too strong and pushes the hand away; the slave leaves the wall, the force disappears late, the hand comes back too far… Each round trip can add energy: the channel behaves like a small motor. That is how a stable system without delay becomes unstable with 100 ms.

6.2 Passivity

A system is passive if it can never give out more energy than it received. Masses, springs, dampers, walls and (roughly) human arms are passive; a connection of passive systems is stable. The problem is the delayed channel: sending a speed one way and a force back with a delay is not passive. The idea of the wave variables is to code the information so that the channel becomes passive again, whatever the delay.

6.3 Wave variables

u = (b ẋm + Fm) / √(2b)    v = (b ẋs − Fs) / √(2b)    b ≈ √(Zm · Zs)

Instead of sending a speed and receiving a force, the master sends the wave u and the slave sends back the wave v; each side rebuilds its speed set point or its force from the wave it receives. The power that crosses the channel is ½u² − ½v²: a delay only shifts the waves in time, it cannot create energy. It is not an architecture but a way of coding the information; b is the wave impedance, a tuning parameter.

Wave variables
✓ teleoperation with delays under control✓ keeps stability (and some transparency) whatever the delay
✗ position driftspeeds are sent, not positions (add a slow position correction)
✗ wave reflectionwaves bounce back and forth, especially if b is badly chosen or if Zmaster is very different from Zslave (fix: impedance matching)

6.4 Other answers to long delays

  • Move and wait: small moves, wait for the image, correct (the lab of lesson 1).
  • Predictive display: the screen shows a simulated robot that answers at once, over the delayed real image.
  • Supervisory control: the operator sends goals, the robot executes them locally with its own loops (shared autonomy). This is exactly the role of the levels of the PAT architecture (lesson 10).

Key terms

  • delay, latency
  • passivity
  • wave variables

The essentials
  • A delay makes the force come back late: the channel can create energy and destabilise the loop.
  • Passive = cannot give out more energy than received; wave variables u = (bẋ + F)/√(2b), v = (bẋ − F)/√(2b) keep the channel passive.
  • Limits: position drift, wave reflection (choose b ≈ √(Z_m Z_s)); other answers: move and wait, predictive display, supervisory control.

Cobotics: the levels of human-robot interaction

You will learn

7.1 A definition

Cobotics is the direct and safe interaction between a human and a robot (J. E. Colgate, 1999; the word cobot was coined by Colgate and Peshkin in 1996). Teleoperation puts a distance between the human and the robot; cobotics brings them together, in the same space, sometimes on the same part.

Cooperationsharedtogetherdifferentthe robot glues one part while the operator assembles another on the same table

The standards on industrial robots (ISO 10218-1 and -2) and ISO/TS 15066 describe four modes of collaborative operation. An application often combines several.

A “cobot” is a robot designed for these modes (force sensing, rounded shapes, limited power). But the application must be assessed as a whole: a cobot holding a knife, a hot glue gun or a heavy part at head height is dangerous. The risk assessment (lessons 11 and 12) is done for the cell, tool and parts included.

Co-manipulation: the hand on the robot

8.1 Three types

In co-manipulation the human and the robot act on the same object at the same time. A. Bahloul (2018) distinguishes three types by where the hand, the robot and the tool are:

TypeWho holds whatExample
Parallelthe hand and the robot (fixed base) both hold the tool or the loadlifting assist for heavy parts, a cobot carrying a car door with the operator
Orthoticthe robot is attached along the limb: an exoskeletonan exoskeleton that helps to hold a tool above the head
Serialthe hand holds the robot, which holds the tool: the robot is between themsurgery: the surgeon holds a handle, the robot holds the instrument

8.2 Four goals

GoalWhat the robot addsTypes
Force amplificationit carries most of the load: the operator only guidesparallel, orthotic
Scalingthe tool moves less (or more) than the hand: big gestures become precise onesserial
Gesture guidingfollow a trajectory, avoid obstacles, do not deviate, reach a given pose (virtual guides, “virtual fixtures”)all three
Filteringremove the parasitic motions: tremor, jerksall three

Two big uses: assistance for physically demanding tasks (handling, overhead work: L. Peternel and colleagues, 2016) and assistance to surgery (guiding and filtering the gesture of the surgeon: N. Zemiti, 2025).

8.3 How the robot follows the hand: admittance control

A force sensor at the handle measures the force of the hand Fh. The controller computes the motion that a virtual object of chosen mass M and damping B would have under this force, and the robot moves that way. Choose a light virtual object and the robot feels light, whatever its real mass.

M ẍ + B ẋ = Fh    (in steady state: ẋ = Fh / B)

  • Amplification: the robot adds (G − 1) times the force of the hand: to accelerate 30 kg at 1 m/s² with a gain of 10, the hand gives 3 N.
  • Scaling: the tool position is the hand displacement divided by G: 20 mm of hand → 4 mm of tool with G = 5.
  • Guiding: a large damping (or a spring) across the path, a small one along it.
  • Filtering: a low-pass filter on the measured force or speed, below the tremor frequency of the hand (about 8 to 12 Hz).

Control architectures: why and how

25 min
You will learn

why a robot application needs an architecture before code, what makes programming a robot special (sampling, real time, parallelism), the role of middleware, and the history of architectures from sense-plan-act to components and models.

9.1 Designing a robotic application

A robotician starts from a mission (a scenario) and an expected behaviour. Between them stand the robot (mechanics, sensors, computers) and its software. Programming “piece by piece” is not enough: algorithms that run together interact in hidden ways and produce artefacts no one planned. So we must design the global architecture before programming the robot: which parts exist, what data flows between them, who decides, at which rate.

9.2 What a robot is for a computer

  • Control theory is continuous (equations in time); a computer is sequential and digital (one instruction after the other, values in steps). We must sample in space and time, which adds problems: aliasing, delay, quantisation.
  • In a robot everything runs at once: joint loops, sensors, planning, communication. On one processor this simultaneity is only pseudo-simultaneity, given by the scheduler of an operating system.
  • Multi-task is not real time: a general-purpose system (Windows, ordinary Linux) runs many tasks but does not guarantee when. A real-time system (VxWorks, QNX, Linux with PREEMPT_RT) guarantees that a 1 ms loop really runs every millisecond.

Robotic middleware (ROS, Orocos, GenoM, RTMaps, Urbi…) is a big help: it moves the data between programs, gives tools and reusable blocks. But it is not enough: it does not tell you how to organise the behaviour. That is the job of the architecture.

9.3 A short history of architectures

FamilyIdeaLimit
Sequential (sense – model – plan – act)one data path: perceive, build a model of the world, plan, then actslow; the plan is old when executed; one failure stops all
Reactive, subsumption (Brooks, 1986)parallel layers of simple behaviours; a higher layer can override (subsume) a lower onefast and robust, but hard to give long-term goals
Discrete eventsfinite-state machines, Petri nets: the robot goes from state to state on eventsexplodes in number of states for big systems
Distributedmodules exchange data through a shared “blackboard” or messagesthe data flow must be managed

Hybrid, by levelsreflexes at the bottom, deliberation at the top (LAAS, PAT…)needs a clear contract between levels

9.4 A state machine, in practice

Most industrial cells are organised as a state machine: wait, pick, place, and a fault state reached from anywhere by an emergency stop or an error. It is easy to read, to test and to certify; ROS 2 offers it with tools such as SMACH or behaviour trees (the modern, modular cousin of state machines, used by Nav2).

Separate the roles

A good practice: the architect designs the scenario and the robot-independent model; the programmers write the algorithms (C, C++, Python, Matlab); the code is then targeted on each robot. The same model can drive several robots.

The PAT architecture: from teleoperation to autonomy

20 min
You will learn

the Perception – Action – Teleoperation architecture of the PRISME laboratory: its levels 0 to 5, the data that flows between them, how an operator can take over at any level, and examples of real robots.

10.1 The idea

Classic architectures are hierarchical, behavioural, hybrid, blackboard or multi-agent, with reactive and deliberative capacities (LAAS, Orccad, AuRA, DAMN, 4D/RCS, CLARAty…). The goal of PAT is a functional framework that covers the whole range between a fully teleoperated robot and a fully autonomous one, with the same blocks: the degree of autonomy becomes a level. Like the OSI model of networks, each level talks to the level below and to its peer.

A robot = Action + Perception + the physical robot (A.M.S.: actuators, mechanics, sensors). The teleoperation part sits beside them.

LevelActionPerceptionSends down
0the robot itself (A.M.S.)physical sensors—
1Servoings: one loop per actuator (PID)Sensors: raw measures (encoders, force)commands to the actuators
2Pilot: reflexes, collision avoidance, inverse modelsProximity model: what is very close (ultrasonic ring, bumpers)setting points (q1, q2…)

10.2 Teleoperation at any level

Beside each level k there is a teleoperation block Tk: the operator can produce what the action block of that level would produce. If the operator works at T3 (gives a trajectory), the robot runs levels 1 and 2 by itself: its autonomy level is 2. Higher autonomy = the operator intervenes higher, with more abstract orders. The level can even change during a mission (dynamic autonomy): take over the pilot in a tricky passage, then give back.

Why it is useful
  • Modular: each block can be changed (another planner, another sensor) without touching the others.
  • Open to many methods of control and perception: complementary, not binary, and dynamic.
  • Usable to design a new robot or to describe an existing one. It is the functional frame; you still need a software frame (OS, real-time kernel, language) and a hardware frame (processors, I/O boards).

11.1 A law obliges, a standard recommends

TextWhat it is
Lawa permanent general obligation, from a sovereign authority; breaking it is punished
European directivean obligation imposed on the States, which transpose it into national law; a large part of French law comes from them
Decree, order (arrêté)acts of the executive that set how a law is applied

Harmonised standarda standard cited in the Official Journal of the EU: following it gives a presumption of conformity with the directive

11.2 The directives of a robot cell

There are two kinds of directives. Market directives (“New Approach”, for new products) set the obligations of manufacturers; social directives set the minimum obligations of employers towards their workers. A robot cell falls under several market directives at once:

  • Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC: the main one.
  • Low Voltage 2014/35/EU and Electromagnetic Compatibility 2014/30/EU: the cabinet, the drives.
  • ATEX 2014/34/EU (it replaced 94/9/EC) if the cell works in an explosive atmosphere (paint, dust).

The New Approach (resolution of 7 May 1985) works the same way for all these texts: the directive sets the essential health and safety requirements (EHSR, in French EESS) — the what; harmonised standards give one way to meet them — the how; adapted certification procedures; and the CE marking at the end. Its aims: free movement of goods in the European Economic Area, safety of workers and of consumers.

From 20 January 2027: the Machinery Regulation

The directive 2006/42/EC (which followed 89/392/EEC and 98/37/EC) will be replaced by the Regulation (EU) 2023/1230. A regulation applies directly in every State, without transposition. The logic stays the same (essential requirements, harmonised standards, CE marking); it adds in particular requirements on cybersecurity, on software and AI whose behaviour evolves, and allows digital instructions.

Safety 2: from risk assessment to CE marking

25 min
You will learn

the five elements of the certification of a machine, the risk assessment and the three-step method, the IDAR method, the required performance level of a safety function, the instructions and the technical file, the CE marking, and what to do with machines already in service.

12.1 Five elements

Who certifies? The manufacturer or its authorised representative — anyone who places the machine on the market. How? For most machines, by self-certification, without a third party. For the dangerous machines listed in Annex IV (certain saws and presses, protective devices that detect persons, safety logic units…), a notified body is involved (EC type-examination or full quality assurance), unless harmonised standards covering all the requirements are fully applied, in which case an internal check is allowed.

12.2 The risk assessment

It is compulsory (Annex I) and kept in the technical file. It follows ISO 12100 and is iterative: determine the limits of the machine → identify the hazards → estimate and evaluate each risk → reduce it → check the result, and loop until the risk is acceptable. It is centred on the activities of the operators during the whole life of the machine, not only on normal production.

Where to start: list the activities

A robot that loads and unloads a machining centre. Normal operation: loading and unloading, finishing, quality control, supply of raw parts, removal of the bin of finished parts. Maintenance: first-level (restart after a fallen part, cleaning, degreasing), second-level (repair of the gripper). For each activity: who is there, where, how often, and what can hurt them (crushing, shearing, impact, projection, energy left in the system after a stop…).

12.3 The three-step method

The order is compulsory (requirement 1.1.2 b): first remove the danger, then protect, and only then inform about what remains.

StepWhatExamples in a robot cell
1. Eliminate or reduce (inherently safe design)change the design so that the danger disappears or shrinksno sharp edges; a layout without crushing zones; a safe trajectory that does not point the tool at the operator; limited force, mass and speed
2. Protect (safeguarding)guards and protective devices for the risks that remainfixed fences; doors with interlocks; light curtains, laser scanners, safety mats; safe zones and safe speeds monitored by the controller
3. Inform (information for use)warn about the residual risksfloor marking of the collaborative zone, warning lights for the mode, instructions, personal protective equipment

12.4 The IDAR method

One method among others to run the risk assessment, in four phases: Describe (the machine and the activities), Analyse (the risk scenarios), Integrate (the safety measures), Report (write the results). It is participative: a multidisciplinary group (design, production, maintenance, safety, operators) brings different but complementary points of view. The group does not have to find a technical solution to every problem; it must see them all.

12.5 How reliable must a safety function be?

A safety function is what the control system does to keep people safe: stop the robot when the door opens, limit the speed in manual mode. ISO 13849-1 asks how reliable it must be — the required performance level PLr, from a to e — from three questions: the severity of the injury (S1 slight, S2 serious), the frequency or duration of exposure (F1, F2), the possibility to avoid the hazard (P1, P2).

For a robot cell, the stop on opening the door is typically S2 (a robot can kill), F2 (operators go in every hour) and P2 (a fast robot cannot be dodged): PLr = e; many cells reach PL d with a slower robot or a reduced exposure. In practice: two channels (redundant contacts, a safety relay or safety controller) with monitoring.

The instructions give three levels of information: information (what the machine is and how it works), advice (how to use it well), and warnings (dangers, safety instructions for each phase). They come from the risk assessment: activities, hazards, instructions to follow, limits of use, and contra-indications against foreseeable misuse.

By the declaration of conformity (Annex II A) the manufacturer states that the machine complies and the signatory commits their responsibility. Then comes the CE marking, last step and only for machines (a partly completed machine has a declaration of incorporation, Annex II B). The plate must show, visibly, legibly and indelibly: the name and full address of the manufacturer (and of the authorised representative), the designation of the machine, the CE marking, the series or type, the serial number if any, and the year of construction (it is forbidden to pre-date or post-date it). The mass of parts to be lifted is advised. The other directives (low voltage, EMC, ATEX) must be met too.

12.7 Machines already in service

A machine already in service is not subject to the Machinery Directive but to the rules on the use of work equipment (directive 89/655/EEC, now 2009/104/EC; in France the decrees 93-40 and 93-41, in the Labour Code). So: do not affix the CE marking again; complete the risk assessment; update the instructions. Beware: a machine substantially modified (new functions, new performance, new risks) or rebuilt from old parts can become a new machine, with the full certification to do again.

Check yourself

  1. In which order must the protective measures be taken?

    1. eliminate or reduce by design, 2. protect (guards, devices), 3. inform about the residual risks.

  2. Must the technical file be given to the customer?

    No: it belongs to the manufacturer and is kept at least 10 years; the instructions, however, must be supplied.

  3. A company modifies an old machine in service, without changing its function. Does it affix a new CE marking?

    No: it completes the risk assessment and updates the instructions (rules for machines in service).

TermMeaning
TeleoperationExtending the senses and movements of a human to a remote environment through a robot.
Master, slaveThe device held by the operator, and the remote robot that reproduces its motion.

Four-channel architectureTeleoperation that sends motion and force in both directions (Lawrence).
PassivityThe property of a system that can never give out more energy than it received.

Co-manipulationA human and a robot acting on the same object at the same time.
