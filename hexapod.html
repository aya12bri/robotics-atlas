Hexapod: the Gough-Stewart platform (6-UPS)

← Robot list · Learn the basics

Six legs that change length (linear actuators) join a fixed base to a moving platform. Each leg has a universal joint at the base and a ball joint at the platform: U-P-S, six times. By changing the six lengths together, the platform moves in its six degrees of freedom: three translations and three rotations. It is a fully parallel robot: one motor per chain, all the motors near the base. Invented by Eric Gough (1954, tyre testing) and made famous by Stewart (1965, flight simulators).

Kinematic diagram (schéma cinématique)

The grey hexagon is the base with its six universal joints (squares), the orange one the platform with its six ball joints (circles), and the lines the legs, numbered 1 to 6. The cross is the centre of the platform.

Inverse model: one line per leg

lᵢ = ‖ p + R(φ, θ, ψ) · bᵢ − aᵢ ‖        i = 1 … 6

aᵢ is the joint of leg i on the base, bᵢ its joint on the platform (in the platform frame), p the position of the centre of the platform and R its rotation (roll φ, pitch θ, yaw ψ). Knowing the pose, each leg is a single distance: six independent lines, always one answer. Compare with a serial robot, whose inverse model is the hard one.

Direct model: Newton's method

The other way round, from six lengths to the pose, has no formula: the equations are coupled and a general Gough-Stewart platform can close in up to 40 different poses. The controller uses Newton's method, starting from the last known pose: Xk+1 = Xk + J−1 (l − G(Xk)), where G is the inverse model above and J its Jacobian. Press the button: the page takes the current six lengths, starts from the home pose, and finds the pose back.



    
Velocities and singularities

    
Each leg speed is the platform velocity projected on the leg: l̇ᵢ = uᵢ · v + (Rbᵢ × uᵢ) · ω, with uᵢ the unit vector of the leg. The six lines form the 6 × 6 matrix J⁻¹ (this is the matrix A⁻¹B of the course, up to a sign). When it becomes singular, the platform gains a motion that the locked legs cannot stop: a parallel singularity. The indicator below measures how far we are from it (1 = perfect, 0 = singular).

    


    

      
Where is a hexapod used?

      
Flight and driving simulators (the cockpit sits on the platform), earthquake and vibration test tables, precision positioning for optics, the secondary mirrors of telescopes and the alignment of particle accelerators (micrometre hexapods by PI, Symétrie), machining heads, and patient positioning in radiotherapy.

      
Strengths: very stiff and accurate (the legs only pull or push, the errors do not add up), a large payload for its own weight, six degrees of freedom. Weaknesses: a small workspace, especially in rotation, coupled motions (every leg moves for any motion), a direct model without formula, and singularities inside the workspace that the controller must avoid.
