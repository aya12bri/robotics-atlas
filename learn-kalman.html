Why fuse data? Sensors for autonomous navigation

15 min
You will learn

what data fusion is for, the sensors of drones, mobile robots and cars, why no sensor is enough alone, the families of estimation methods, and the practical problems (calibration, biases, rates, faults).

In one sentence

Data fusion combines several imperfect sensors and a model of motion to get one estimate of the state of a robot that is better than any sensor alone — and it also says how sure it is.

1.1 The problem: autonomous navigation

A drone (UAV), an autonomous mobile robot (AMR) or a self-driving car must know at every instant its state: position, speed, orientation (up to 6D). Safety requires redundancy: several sensors measure the same thing in different ways, so that one failure does not blind the robot.

SensorWhat it measuresWeak point
IMU: accelerometers, gyroscopes, magnetometersacceleration (gravity + own acceleration), rotation speed, Earth magnetic field, in the body frameintegration drifts; magnetic disturbances
GNSS (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou)position from pseudo-ranges (multilateration)outdoors only, multipath, a few metres without corrections
Barometer, Pitot tubealtitude, air speedweather, wind
Lidar (time of flight)distances to landmarks and obstaclesglass, rain, cost
UWB radio (ultra wide band)distance by time of flight (TOF), angle of arrival (AOA), differences of time or phase (TDOA, PDOA)needs installed anchors, multipath
Wheel encoderswheel rotation → odometryslip, drift
Cameras (RGB, depth, event)images, depth by IR, time of flight or stereo, visual SLAMlight, texture, heavy computing

1.2 Three families of methods

  • Filtering: Kalman filter (linear), extended Kalman filter (EKF), complementary filter, unscented Kalman filter (UKF), invariant Kalman filter, particle filter. Recursive and fast: the subject of this course.
  • Nonlinear optimisation: maximum a posteriori over a window of past states (Bayesian networks, factor graphs, iSAM with incremental updates): the standard of visual-inertial and GNSS-inertial-vision fusion.
  • Learning: model-free, data-driven (deep networks, LSTM), for example to estimate depth or to correct the drift of an IMU.

1.3 What makes it hard in practice

  • Calibration: biases that change with time and temperature, misalignment between IMU and camera.
  • Observability: can the sensors really tell us every component of the state? (a compass alone never gives the position).
  • Asynchronous sampling: an IMU at 400 Hz, a GNSS at 5 Hz, a camera at 30 Hz with a delay.
  • Resilience: detect a faulty sensor, switch, keep running (fault tolerance).

Random vectors, covariance and confidence ellipses

25 min
You will learn

mean and covariance of random vectors, whiteness, the properties of covariance matrices, the confidence ellipse and the χ² law, and how a linear map transforms a covariance: Γy = A Γx Aᵀ + Γα.

In one sentence

A covariance matrix says how much each component of an uncertain vector varies, and how the components vary together; it draws an ellipse of uncertainty.

2.1 Mean, variations, covariance

For random vectors x ∈ ℝⁿ and y ∈ ℝᵐ, the means are x̄ = E(x), ȳ = E(y) and the variations x̃ = x − x̄, ỹ = y − ȳ. The covariance matrix is the mean of the products of the variations:

Γxy = E(x̃ ỹᵀ) = E((x − x̄)(y − ȳ)ᵀ)    Γx = Γxx = E(x̃ x̃ᵀ)

On the diagonal of Γx: the variances σᵢ² of each component. Off the diagonal: the covariances, which say if two components tend to move together. A vector is white if its components are independent: Γx is then diagonal. In Matlab or Python: subtract the mean of the samples, then average the products (G_xy = mean(xtilde .* ytilde)).

Uncorrelated is not independent

Points on a ring (x = ρ sin θ, y = ρ cos θ) have a zero covariance, yet x and y are strongly linked: knowing x gives two possible y. The covariance only sees linear links.

2.2 Properties of a covariance matrix

  • It is symmetric and positive: its eigenvalues are real and ≥ 0.
  • Decomposition: Γ = R D Rᵀ, R a rotation (the eigenvectors), D diagonal (the eigenvalues).
  • Square root: Γ½ = R D½ Rᵀ, itself a covariance matrix.
  • Order: Γ₁ ≤ Γ₂ means Γ₂ − Γ₁ is positive: Γ₁ is “more precise” in every direction.

2.3 The confidence ellipse

For a Gaussian vector w of mean w̄ and covariance Γw, the set (w − w̄)ᵀ Γw⁻¹ (w − w̄) ≤ a² is an ellipse: the image of the unit disk by w = w̄ + a Γw½ s. The quantity sᵀs follows a χ² law with 2 degrees of freedom, so the probability to be inside is η = 1 − e−a²/2, hence:

a = √(−2 ln(1 − η))    η = 90 % → a = 2.15   η = 95 % → a = 2.45   η = 99 % → a = 3.03

2.4 How a linear map moves the uncertainty

If y = A x + α + b, with A and b known, x and α independent and α centred, then:

ȳ = A x̄ + b    Γy = A Γx Aᵀ + Γα

This single line is the heart of the prediction step of the Kalman filter. It also tells how to generate any Gaussian vector: take a white unit vector s (x̄ = 0, Γ = I), then y = Γy½ s + ȳ has the mean ȳ and the covariance Γy.

Worked example

x has Γx = I (σ = 1 on each axis) and y = A x with A = [2 0 ; 0 1]. Then Γy = A I Aᵀ = [4 0 ; 0 1]: the standard deviation of y₁ is 2. With A a rotation of 45°, Γy = I: a circle stays a circle when it turns.

Key terms

  • random vector
  • covariance matrix
  • white vector
  • eigenvalues, eigenvectors
  • confidence ellipse
  • χ² law
  • Γy = A Γx Aᵀ + Γα
The essentials
  • Γx = E((x − x̄)(x − x̄)ᵀ): symmetric, positive; Γ = R D Rᵀ (eigenvectors, eigenvalues).
  • Confidence ellipse: half-axes a√λᵢ along the eigenvectors, a = √(−2 ln(1 − η)) in 2D.
  • y = A x + α + b ⇒ ȳ = A x̄ + b, Γy = A Γx Aᵀ + Γα.

The best linear estimator: unbiased and orthogonal

25 min
You will learn

what an unbiased orthogonal estimator is, why it is unique and the best of all linear estimators, the five formulas for y = C x + β, and two worked examples: a DC motor and an over-determined linear system.

In one sentence

Knowing y, the best linear guess of x moves the prior guess x̄ in the direction of the surprise y − ȳ, with a gain K = Γxy Γy⁻¹ that weighs how much y really tells about x.

3.1 Unbiased and orthogonal

A linear estimator of x from y is x̂ = K y + b. Its error is ε = x̂ − x. It is unbiased if E(ε) = 0 (no systematic error), orthogonal if E(ε ỹᵀ) = 0 (the error has nothing left in common with the measurement: everything y could tell has been used).

x̂ = x̄ + K (y − ȳ)    K = Γxy Γy⁻¹    Γε = Γx − K Γyx

Proof in three lines: unbiased ⇒ b = x̄ − K ȳ, so ε = K ỹ − x̃; orthogonal ⇒ E((K ỹ − x̃) ỹᵀ) = K Γy − Γxy = 0 ⇒ K = Γxy Γy⁻¹. And it is the best: any other gain K₀ + Δ adds the positive term Δ Γy Δᵀ to the error covariance.

3.2 The case y = C x + β

When the measurement is a linear function of x plus a centred noise β uncorrelated with x, Γxy = Γx Cᵀ and Γy = C Γx Cᵀ + Γβ. The five formulas to remember:

FormulaName
(i)x̂ = x̄ + K ỹestimate
(ii)Γε = Γx − K C Γxerror covariance
(iii)ỹ = y − C x̄innovation
(iv)Γy = C Γx Cᵀ + Γβinnovation covariance
(v)K = Γx Cᵀ Γy⁻¹Kalman gain
Worked example: the DC motor

The speed obeys Ω = x₁ U + x₂ Tr (U voltage, Tr load torque). Five tests give (U, Tr, Ω) = (4, 0, 5), (10, 1, 10), (10, 5, 8), (13, 5, 14), (15, 3, 17); measurement variance 9; a priori x ≈ (1, −1) with variance 4. Each row of C is (U, Tr). The formulas give x̂ = (1.21, −0.58) and Γε = [0.061 −0.166 ; −0.166 0.598]: x₁ is known within ±0.25, x₂ only within ±0.77 (the tests vary Tr little).

Worked example: three equations, two unknowns

2x₁ + 3x₂ = 8, 3x₁ + 2x₂ = 7, x₁ − x₂ = 0, the first one twice as accurate (Γβ = diag(1, 4, 4)), and a vague prior x̄ = 0, Γx = 1000 I. The estimator makes a weighted compromise: x̂ = (1.311, 1.755), Γε = [0.728 −0.515 ; −0.515 0.462]. It is weighted least squares, with an uncertainty.

  • unbiased
  • orthogonal
  • innovation
  • Kalman gain
  • error covariance
  • weighted least squares
The essentials
  • x̂ = x̄ + K (y − ȳ), K = Γxy Γy⁻¹, Γε = Γx − K Γyx: unique, unbiased, orthogonal, the best linear estimator.
  • For y = C x + β: ỹ = y − C x̄, Γy = C Γx Cᵀ + Γβ, K = Γx Cᵀ Γy⁻¹, Γε = Γx − K C Γx.
  • DC motor: x̂ = (1.21, −0.58). Over-determined system: x̂ = (1.311, 1.755).

The Kalman filter: predict, then correct

25 min
You will learn

the state model, the two steps of the filter and their seven equations, what the filter stores, its code in a few lines, and why it gives the same result as the batch estimator on the DC motor.

In one sentence

The Kalman filter applies the best linear estimator again and again: the model predicts where the state goes (and the uncertainty grows), then each measurement corrects it (and the uncertainty shrinks).

4.1 The model

xk+1 = Ak xk + uk + αk    yk = Ck xk + βk

αk (process noise, covariance Γα: what the model ignores) and βk (measurement noise, Γβ) are Gaussian, independent of each other and white in time (independent from one instant to the next). x̂k|k−1 reads “the estimate of x at time k knowing the measurements up to k − 1”.

StepEquationName
Correctionỹₖ = yₖ − Cₖ x̂ₖ|ₖ₋₁innovation
Sₖ = Cₖ Γₖ|ₖ₋₁ Cₖᵀ + Γβinnovation covariance
Kₖ = Γₖ|ₖ₋₁ Cₖᵀ Sₖ⁻¹Kalman gain
x̂ₖ|ₖ = x̂ₖ|ₖ₋₁ + Kₖ ỹₖcorrected estimate
Γₖ|ₖ = (I − Kₖ Cₖ) Γₖ|ₖ₋₁corrected covariance
Predictionx̂ₖ₊₁|ₖ = Aₖ x̂ₖ|ₖ + uₖpredicted estimate
Γₖ₊₁|ₖ = Aₖ Γₖ|ₖ Aₖᵀ + Γαpredicted covariance

4.2 The code

The filter only stores x̂k+1|k and Γk+1|k; everything else is computed on the fly. The same function in Matlab (as in the course) and in Python:

function [x1,G1]=kalman(x0,G0,u,y,G_alpha,G_beta,A,C)
  S=C*G0*C'+G_beta;  K=G0*C'*inv(S);  y_tilde=y-C*x0;
  xup=x0+K*y_tilde;  Gup=G0-K*C*G0;          % correction
  x1=A*xup+u;        G1=A*Gup*A'+G_alpha;    % prediction
end
import numpy as np
def kalman(x0, G0, u, y, Ga, Gb, A, C):
    S = C @ G0 @ C.T + Gb
    K = G0 @ C.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)
    xup = x0 + K @ (y - C @ x0)
    Gup = (np.eye(len(x0)) - K @ C) @ G0
    return A @ xup + u, A @ Gup @ A.T + Ga
The DC motor, one measurement at a time

Take A = I, u = 0, Γα = 0 (the parameters do not change) and Ck = (U(k), Tr(k)): feeding the five tests one by one gives exactly the result of the batch estimator of lesson 3. With Γα = 0 and A = I, the Kalman filter is the recursive form of the linear estimator. Use the lab of lesson 3, button “Add the next measurement”, to see it.

A numerical safeguard

With rounding errors Γk|k can lose its positivity. A more robust form (Joseph form) is Γk|k = (I − K C) Γ (I − K C)ᵀ + K Γβ Kᵀ, always symmetric positive.

Key terms

  • state model
  • process noise Γα
  • measurement noise Γβ
  • prediction, correction
  • innovation covariance
  • white in time
  • Joseph form
The essentials
  • Model: xk+1 = A xk + u + α, y = C x + β, noises white, Gaussian, independent.
  • Correction: ỹ, S, K, x̂ ← x̂ + K ỹ, Γ ← (I − K C) Γ. Prediction: x̂ ← A x̂ + u, Γ ← A Γ Aᵀ + Γα.
  • With A = I, Γα = 0 the filter is the recursive form of the batch estimator.

The asymptotic filter: tuning Γα and Γβ

20 min
You will learn

the steady state of the Kalman filter on the temperature of a room, the formulas of Γ∞ and K∞, what happens when Γα or Γβ goes to zero, how to tune a filter, the whiteness of the innovation and the 99 % confidence ellipsoid.

In one sentence

After a while the gain of a Kalman filter stops changing: it settles on a value that only depends on the ratio between how fast the state moves (Γα) and how noisy the sensor is (Γβ).

5.1 The temperature of a room

xk+1 = xk + αk    yk = xk + βk

Here A = C = 1. One step of the filter (correction then prediction) gives the recursion of the predicted variance:

x̂k+1|k = x̂k|k−1 + Kk (yk − x̂k|k−1)   Kk = Γk|k−1 / (Γk|k−1 + Γβ)   Γk+1|k = Γk|k−1 Γβ / (Γk|k−1 + Γβ) + Γα

When k is large, Γk+1|k = Γk|k−1 = Γ∞. Solving Γ∞ = Γ∞Γβ / (Γ∞ + Γβ) + Γα gives Γ∞² − Γα Γ∞ − Γα Γβ = 0, so:

Γ∞ = (Γα + √(Γα² + 4 Γα Γβ)) / 2    K∞ = Γ∞ / (Γ∞ + Γβ)    precision: Γ∞ Γβ / (Γ∞ + Γβ)

CaseResultMeaning
Γα → 0Γ∞ → 0, K∞ → 0the temperature does not move: the filter ends up ignoring new measurements and averages all of them
Γβ → 0Γ∞ → Γα, K∞ → 1the thermometer is perfect: the filter copies the measurement
Γα = ΓβΓ∞ = 1.618 Γα, K∞ = 0.618a balanced compromise… and the golden ratio!

5.2 Tuning a filter in practice

  • Γβ comes from the datasheet or from a test: leave the sensor still and compute the variance of its readings.
  • Γα is the honest price of the simplifications of the model; it is the real tuning knob: too small → the filter is slow and can diverge if the model is wrong; too large → it follows the noise.
  • Check: the innovation ỹk of a well-tuned filter is white (no correlation in time) with covariance Sk; the normalised innovation ỹᵀ S⁻¹ ỹ should average the size of y.
A 99 % confidence ellipsoid

At time k the state is inside {x : (x − x̂k|k)ᵀ Γk|k⁻¹ (x − x̂k|k) ≤ a²} with a² the 99 % quantile of the χ² law with n degrees of freedom: 9.21 for n = 2, 11.34 for n = 3. In 2D, a = √(−2 ln 0.01) = 3.03.

Key terms

  • asymptotic (steady-state) Kalman filter
  • Riccati equation
  • filter tuning
  • innovation whiteness
  • confidence ellipsoid
The essentials
  • Room temperature: Γ∞ = (Γα + √(Γα² + 4ΓαΓβ)) / 2, K∞ = Γ∞ / (Γ∞ + Γβ).
  • Γα → 0: K → 0 (average); Γβ → 0: K → 1 (copy); Γα = Γβ: K = 0.618.
  • Tune Γβ from the sensor, Γα as the price of the model; check that the innovation is white.

The extended Kalman filter (EKF)

25 min
You will learn

how to apply Kalman to a nonlinear model by linearising it around the estimate, the equations of the EKF, the example of the simple pendulum seen by a gyrometer, and why the EKF is no longer optimal.

In one sentence

Real models are curved; the EKF replaces the curve by its tangent at the current estimate and runs the ordinary Kalman filter on it, step after step.

6.1 Linearise around the estimate

xk+1 = f(xk, uk) + αk    yk = g(xk) + βk

Near x̂, f(x) ≈ f(x̂) + A (x − x̂) and g(x) ≈ g(x̂) + C (x − x̂), with the Jacobians Ak = ∂f/∂x at (x̂k|k, uk) and Ck = ∂g/∂x at x̂k|k−1. The linear filter applied to this tangent model simplifies into:

EKF
Predictionx̂ₖ₊₁|ₖ = f(x̂ₖ|ₖ, uₖ) , Γₖ₊₁|ₖ = Aₖ Γₖ|ₖ Aₖᵀ + Γα
Innovationz̃ₖ = yₖ − g(x̂ₖ|ₖ₋₁) , Sₖ = Cₖ Γₖ|ₖ₋₁ Cₖᵀ + Γβ
CorrectionKₖ = Γₖ|ₖ₋₁ Cₖᵀ Sₖ⁻¹ , x̂ₖ|ₖ = x̂ₖ|ₖ₋₁ + Kₖ z̃ₖ , Γₖ|ₖ = (I − Kₖ Cₖ) Γₖ|ₖ₋₁

Notice: the estimate uses the true nonlinear functions f and g; only the covariances use the Jacobians.

6.2 Example: the simple pendulum

In normalised form, x₁ is the angle and x₂ the rotation speed: ẋ₁ = x₂, ẋ₂ = −sin x₁ + u (u known). A gyrometer measures x₂ every 0.01 s with a noise σ = 0.1 rad/s. Euler’s method (δ = 0.01 s) gives the discrete model and its Jacobian:

x₁⁺ = x₁ + δ x₂   x₂⁺ = x₂ + δ(−sin x₁ + u)    A = [1 δ ; −δ cos x̂₁ 1]   C = [0 1]   Γβ = 0.01

The gyrometer never measures the angle, yet the filter finds it: the dynamics link them (the acceleration −sin x₁ depends on the angle). We say the angle is observable through the model.

Is the EKF still the best unbiased orthogonal estimator?

No. The linearisation is an approximation: the estimate can be biased, the covariance can be too optimistic, and if the initial error is large (outside the zone where the tangent is a good copy), the filter can diverge. Remedies: a good initialisation, a larger Γα, the unscented filter (UKF) or a particle filter for strong nonlinearities.

Key terms

  • extended Kalman filter (EKF)
  • linearisation
  • Jacobian
  • Euler discretisation
  • observable state
  • divergence
  • UKF, particle filter
The essentials
  • EKF: predict with f, correct with g; covariances with the Jacobians A = ∂f/∂x, C = ∂g/∂x at the estimate.
  • Pendulum: A = [1 δ ; −δ cos x̂₁ 1], C = [0 1]: the angle is found from the gyro through the dynamics.
  • Not optimal any more: bias, optimistic covariance, divergence if the initial error is large.

7

Localising a robot by multilateration (UWB)

25 min
You will learn

the kinematic model of a mobile robot in discrete time, the distance measurement to a beacon and its Jacobian, the data needed to run the EKF, and why the heading becomes observable when the robot moves.

In one sentence

Beacons at known places measure their distance to the robot; the EKF mixes these distances with the wheel speeds to know the position and the heading at every instant.

7.1 The model

The robot (x, y, θ) moves with a known translation speed u and rotation speed ω: ẋ = u cos θ, ẏ = u sin θ, θ̇ = ω. With Euler’s method and the step Δt:

xk+1 = xk + Δt uk cos θk   yk+1 = yk + Δt uk sin θk   θk+1 = θk + Δt ωk

Ak = [1 0 −Δt u sin θ̂ ; 0 1 Δt u cos θ̂ ; 0 0 1]

7.2 The measurement

A UWB anchor Bi = (xbi, ybi) measures the time of flight, hence the distance, every 20 ms with a variance of 25·10⁻⁴ m² (σ = 5 cm):

di = √((x − xbi)² + (y − ybi)²)    Ci = [ (x̂ − xbi)/d̂i   (ŷ − ybi)/d̂i   0 ]

The row Ci is the unit vector from the beacon to the robot: a distance only corrects the position along this line. Its last term is 0: a distance says nothing directly about θ. Yet θ is observable through the motion: if θ is wrong, the predicted positions drift sideways and the distances correct them, which corrects θ through the correlations of Γ.

Data needed to run the EKFHere
positions of the beaconsmeasured once, precisely
inputs u, ω and their noisefrom the wheel encoders → Γα
measurement varianceΓβ = 25·10⁻⁴ m² per distance
step Δt20 ms
initial x̂₀ and Γ₀a rough guess with a large Γ₀
Process the distances one by one

When the noises of the beacons are independent, the three distances can be used as three successive scalar corrections: no matrix inversion, and a beacon that drops out is simply skipped. It is how the lab works.

Key terms

  • multilateration
  • time of flight (TOF)
  • UWB anchor
  • unicycle model
  • measurement Jacobian
  • sequential correction
The essentials
  • Unicycle: x⁺ = x + Δt u cos θ, y⁺ = y + Δt u sin θ, θ⁺ = θ + Δt ω.
  • Distance to beacon i: C = ((x̂ − xᵢ)/d̂, (ŷ − yᵢ)/d̂, 0): corrects along the line beacon → robot.
  • θ is observable through the motion; 3 non-aligned beacons fix the position.

8

Multimodal fusion: several sensors, several rates

25 min
You will learn

how to fuse a compass, angulation, TDOA and odometry, the measurement equations and Jacobians of each, which modality sees which part of the state, how to handle sensors that do not work at the same rate, and centralised against distributed fusion.

In one sentence

Each sensor gives a partial view; the filter predicts at the rate of the fastest source and adds every measurement at the moment it arrives, so that together they see the whole state.

8.1 The three modalities

The robot follows xk+1 = xk + u cos θ, yk+1 = yk + u sin θ, θk+1 = θk + v (u, v from the odometry every 200 ms, not perfectly known: add a process noise). Four beacons: B₁ (−10, −10), B₂ (10, −10), B₃ (10, 10), B₄ (−10, 10) m.

ModalityMeasuresEveryVarianceRow of CSees
M · compass (IMU)θ200 ms4·10⁻⁴ rad²(0, 0, 1)the heading only
B · angulationbearing of beacon l: γ = atan2(yl − y, xl − x) − θ600 ms3.8·10⁻² rad²(Δy/q, −Δx/q, −1)position and heading (≥ 2 beacons)
T · TDOAΔd(i, j) = d(M, Bᵢ) − d(M, Bⱼ)400 ms10⁻² m²difference of the two unit vectors, 0the position only (≥ 3 beacons)

(Δx, Δy = beacon − robot, q = Δx² + Δy². TDOA needs no clock on the robot: only the difference of the times of arrival counts, which is why it is used with UWB and GNSS.)

8.2 Different rates: predict at the base rate, correct when it arrives

  1. Choose a base step (here 200 ms, the rate of the odometry) and predict at every step.
  2. At each step, correct with every measurement that arrived during the step (compass every step, TDOA every 2 steps, angulation every 3 steps), one after the other.
  3. If a measurement arrives late (a camera with 100 ms of processing), correct the state of the moment it was taken (keep a short history), or at least add its delay to its noise.

8.3 Centralised or distributed

In a centralised filter (the lab), every raw measurement enters one filter: optimal, but everything depends on one computer. In a distributed implementation, each modality runs its own local filter; a fusion centre combines the local estimates. For two independent estimates (x₁, Γ₁) and (x₂, Γ₂) of the same state:

Γ = (Γ₁⁻¹ + Γ₂⁻¹)⁻¹    x̂ = Γ (Γ₁⁻¹ x₁ + Γ₂⁻¹ x₂)

Beware of the common information

The local filters share the same odometry, so their errors are correlated: the formula above would then be too optimistic. Fusion centres use the information form with the common prediction removed, or the covariance intersection, which stays safe when the correlation is unknown.

  • multimodal fusion
  • angulation (angle of arrival)
  • TDOA
  • asynchronous measurements
  • centralised, distributed fusion
  • covariance intersection
The essentials
  • Compass sees θ; angulation sees x, y, θ; TDOA sees x, y; odometry predicts.
  • Different rates: predict at the base rate, correct with each measurement when it arrives.
  • Distributed fusion: Γ = (Γ₁⁻¹ + Γ₂⁻¹)⁻¹ if independent; otherwise remove the common part or use covariance intersection.
