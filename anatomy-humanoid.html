Definition

A humanoid robot has the shape of a human body: two legs, a torso, two arms with hands and a head, with about 20 to 50 joints. It is made to work in places and with tools designed for people. Its hardest problem is balance: when it walks it stands on one foot at a time and must keep its centre of mass under control at every instant.

Pose and walking

Move the sliders to crouch, twist the waist, raise the arms or turn the head. Press Walk to make it walk; the pace slider sets the step length and the rhythm.

Each leg has about 6 joints: 3 at the hip, 1 at the knee and 2 at the ankle. The strongest motors are here, because the legs carry the whole weight and absorb the impact of the steps. Seen in X-ray: motors, ball screws and linear actuators.

A joint that twists and leans the upper body. Twisting the waist against the legs cancels the spin that each step gives to the body, like a person swinging their arms.

Three joints at the shoulder, one at the elbow (and often two at the wrist). The arms balance the walk and do the work: carrying, pushing, opening doors.

Five fingers (a thumb and four fingers) moved by small motors placed in the forearm and pulled by tendons (seen in X-ray). Dexterous hands have 12 to more than 20 degrees of freedom and tactile sensors.

Stereo cameras (depth), microphones and often a lidar. The neck turns the head so that the sensors can look around while the body walks.

The battery sits in the torso, in the middle of the body, and gives 1 to 2 hours of autonomy. The computer runs the balance loop (1 kHz) and, on a second board, vision and planning. Seen in X-ray.

What is inside each joint?

  • About 30 actuators: a brushless motor with a harmonic or planetary gearbox at each joint, or an electric linear actuator (motor and ball screw) for the knee and the ankle. Hydraulic humanoids use oil instead.
  • Sensors for balance: an IMU in the pelvis (attitude and acceleration), an encoder on every joint, and force and torque sensors in the feet that tell where the load is.
  • Walking control: a footstep planner, a model of the body as an inverted pendulum (the centre of mass and the zero-moment point), then whole-body inverse kinematics and joint torque control at 1 kHz.
  • Energy and heat: walking uses a lot of power even when standing still, because the leg motors have to hold the weight; the battery and the motors need cooling.

The humanoid really walks: a gait generator moves each foot (backwards under the body while it is on the ground, forwards in the air) and the two-link inverse kinematics of the leg finds the hip and knee angles that put the ankle there; the ankle then keeps the foot flat. When it stands, the hips move so that the centre of mass (computed from the mass of every part) stays over the feet: crouch and raise an arm to see it. When it walks, the centre of mass leaves the support area at each step, so the balance is dynamic, a controlled fall; real humanoids add feedback from the IMU and the force sensors (ZMP or model predictive control). The model is simplified: the real inside depends on the make.
