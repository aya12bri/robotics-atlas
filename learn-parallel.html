1

Locating the tool: joint space and operational space

20 min
You will learn

how to give the position and the orientation of a tool, the two spaces in which a robot lives, the direct and inverse geometric models that link them, and why a straight line in one space is a curve in the other.

In one sentence

A robot has two descriptions of the same posture: the values of its joints (what the motors know) and the position and orientation of its tool (what the task needs); the geometric models translate one into the other.

1.1 Locating an object in space

To locate an object you give two things in a reference frame R₀ attached to the world: the position of one chosen point of the object (its centre of gravity, a corner, the tip of a tool) — three coordinates x, y, z — and the orientation of a frame R₁ attached to the object — three angles. Six numbers in all: the six degrees of freedom of a free solid.

ConventionThe three rotationsUsed for
Euler ZXZψ about Z, then θ about the new X, then φ about the new Z: R = R(Z, ψ) R(X, θ) R(Z, φ)tops, wrists of robots, mechanics
Cardan (roll, pitch, yaw)γ about X, β about Y, α about Z, all fixed axes: R = R(Z, α) R(Y, β) R(X, γ)drones, boats, cars, mobile robots
The order matters

Rotations do not commute: turning 90° about X then 90° about Z does not give the same result as the other way round. And every three-angle convention has a posture where two axes line up (β = ±90° for Cardan): one angle is lost, the gimbal lock. The orientation lesson of the maths course lets you try it.

1.2 Two spaces for one posture

Take a cylindrical robot with three joints: q₁ turns the column, q₂ lifts the arm, q₃ pushes it out. Its posture is perfectly known by the three numbers (q₁, q₂, q₃): one point Q in the joint space. The same posture puts the tool at one point E = (x, y, z) of the operational space (also called task space or Cartesian space). Q and E are two pictures of the same configuration.

  • The joint space has dimension n, the number of joints: 4 for a SCARA, 6 for a 6R arm, 7 for a KUKA LBR. Beyond 3 we cannot draw it, but the computer handles it the same way.
  • When the robot moves, Q draws a trajectory in the joint space and E draws one in the operational space: two descriptions of one task. The programmer usually thinks in E; the motors only understand Q.
  • The direct geometric model (DGM, French MGD) computes E from Q; the inverse geometric model (IGM, French MGI) computes Q from E. The modelling course builds both for real arms (and its lesson 7 revisits these two spaces).

1.3 The two-link arm, our guide for the whole course

Keep only the first two joints of a SCARA: two arms of lengths L₁ and L₂ that turn in a horizontal plane. Its DGM is two lines of trigonometry:

x = L₁ cos θ₁ + L₂ cos(θ₁ + θ₂)    y = L₁ sin θ₁ + L₂ sin(θ₁ + θ₂)

Its IGM comes from the law of cosines: cos θ₂ = (x² + y² − L₁² − L₂²) / (2 L₁ L₂), which gives two values ±θ₂ (elbow on one side or the other), then θ₁ = atan2(y, x) − atan2(L₂ sin θ₂, L₁ + L₂ cos θ₂).

Worked example: one straight line, two paths

L₁ = L₂ = 1 m. Start A: θ = (0°, 90°) → x = 1 + cos 90° = 1, y = 0 + sin 90° = 1, so A = (1, 1). End B: θ = (90°, 0°) → B = (0, 2).

Halfway on the straight line of the joint space: θ = (45°, 45°) → x = cos 45° + cos 90° = 0.707, y = sin 45° + sin 90° = 1.707. Halfway on the straight line of the plane: (0.5, 1.5). The two midpoints are √(0.207² + 0.207²) ≈ 0.29 m apart: the joint path bulges outwards. A straight line in one space is a curve in the other.

What to try in the lab
  • Choose “straight in joint space” and move B: the yellow path in the plane bends.
  • Choose “straight in the plane”: now the path in the joint space bends — and can even leave the joint limits.
  • Drag A across the line θ₂ = 0: the aspect changes from + to −.

Key terms

  • joint space
  • operational space
  • direct geometric model (DGM)
  • inverse geometric model (IGM)
  • Euler angles ZXZ
  • roll, pitch, yaw
The essentials
  • A posture has two pictures: Q in the joint space (q₁ … qₙ) and E in the operational space (position + orientation).
  • DGM: E = f(Q); IGM: Q = g(E). 2-link arm: x = L₁ cos θ₁ + L₂ cos(θ₁ + θ₂), y = L₁ sin θ₁ + L₂ sin(θ₁ + θ₂).
  • A straight line in one space is in general a curve in the other.

From joint limits to the real workspace

20 min
You will learn

why every joint has stops, how the rectangle of the joint limits becomes the shape of the workspace, the role of the aspects, and how to read a workspace drawing in a robot datasheet.

In one sentence

The workspace is the image of the joint limits through the direct model: draw the border of the joint box, transform it, and you get the border of what the tool can reach.

2.1 Every joint has stops

Cables, hoses, the body of the robot and mechanical end stops forbid full turns: each joint variable lives in an interval qi,min ≤ qᵢ ≤ qi,max. In the joint space the allowed postures form a box (a rectangle for two joints). Each point inside the box is a posture; the workspace is the set of all the tool positions these postures produce.

RobotJointsShape of the workspace
Cartesian (gantry)3 slidersa rectangular box: the joint box itself
Cylindrical1 pivot + 2 slidersa thick cylinder sector
SCARA2 pivots in a plane + 1 slidera crescent, extruded vertically
6-axis anthropomorphic6 pivotsa “mushroom” or a thick sphere with holes

The more pivots, the more curved the borders. Datasheets draw only the volume reached by the centre of the wrist (or of the tool flange): adding the orientation would need six dimensions.

2.2 The method: transform the borders

  1. Draw the joint box: for the OMRON Cobra SCARA, −105° ≤ θ₁ ≤ 105° and −157.5° ≤ θ₂ ≤ 157.5°.
  2. Walk around its four borders and transform each point with the DGM: each border becomes a curve in the plane.
  3. Add the image of the line θ₂ = 0 (arm stretched): it separates the two aspects, elbow on the right (θ₂ < 0) and elbow on the left (θ₂ > 0).
  4. Overlap all these curves: the outer envelope is the workspace; inside, some zones are reached with both elbows, others with one only.
Worked example: the hole in the middle

L₁ = L₂ = 1 m (Cobra limits). The farthest point is at L₁ + L₂ = 2 m (θ₂ = 0). The closest is reached with the elbow folded to its stop, θ₂ = 157.5°: by the law of cosines rmin = √(L₁² + L₂² + 2 L₁ L₂ cos θ₂) = √(2 + 2 cos 157.5°) = √(2 − 1.848) ≈ 0.39 m. Without the stop (θ₂ = 180°) the tool could reach the base axis itself; with it, a disc of 0.39 m around the base is out of reach.

And the back of the robot? θ₁ is limited to ±105°: with the elbow stretched, the tool cannot go behind the base beyond 105°. But with the elbow folded, the forearm points backwards: the crescent reaches further round than ±105°. This is why the real Cobra workspace looks like a crescent with rounded horns.

Reachable is not reachable from everywhere

A point inside the workspace may be reachable with the left elbow only. If the robot is in the right aspect, going there needs a change of aspect: the arm passes through the stretched posture θ₂ = 0, a singularity (the singularities course explains why the arm cannot do that along a straight line).

Key terms

  • joint limit, stop
  • workspace
  • aspect
  • boundary of the workspace
The essentials
  • Joint limits make a box in the joint space; its image by the DGM is the workspace.
  • Method: transform the borders of the box and the line θ₂ = 0, then take the envelope.
  • r_max = L₁ + L₂; r_min = √(L₁² + L₂² + 2 L₁ L₂ cos θ₂,max).

Three ways to move: linear, joint, and linear at constant speed

20 min
You will learn

the three motion modes of an industrial controller, which space imposes the trajectory in each, what they cost in time and accuracy, and how to choose them in RAPID (ABB) and KRL (KUKA).

In one sentence

Choosing a motion mode means choosing which space draws the path: the operational space (straight lines, exact but constrained), the joint space (free curves, fast and easy for the motors), or a mix (straight sides, rounded corners, never stopping).

3.1 The task: draw a triangle with the tool

ModeWhat happensWho imposesInstructions
Linearthe tool follows each side exactly, at constant speed, but must stop at each corner (zero speed) before leaving in a new directionthe operational space imposes the joint motionABB MoveL … fine, KUKA LIN
Joint (point to point)only the corners count; between them all joints move together in the way that is best for the motors; the sides are curvesthe joint space induces the operational pathABB MoveJ, KUKA PTP
Linear at constant speed (blending)straight sides, but near each corner a deviation is allowed: the tool cuts the corner on an arc and never stopsoperational on the sides, joint at the cornersABB MoveL … z10, KUKA LIN … C_DIS

Why care? For gluing or sealing, a stop leaves a blob of glue: the third mode is mandatory. For moving between two places (pick and place), the joint mode is the fastest and never meets a singularity on the way. For cutting or welding along an edge, the linear mode is required — and its path must stay away from singularities and joint limits, which you must check.

Worked example: the price of the stops

Triangle of sides 0.3 m, top speed v = 0.2 m/s, acceleration a = 1 m/s². With stops, each side is a trapezoid of speed: accelerate for v/a = 0.2 s, cruise, brake for 0.2 s. Time per side = d/v + v/a = 1.5 + 0.2 = 1.7 s, so 5.1 s for the triangle.

With blending the tool never brakes: about perimeter / v = 0.9 / 0.2 = 4.5 s (a little less, since the arcs are shorter than the corners they cut). 12 % faster — and no blob of glue.

What to try in the lab
  • Linear: watch the speed curve fall to zero at each corner.
  • Joint: the sides bulge; the “largest deviation” readout gives the error in mm.
  • Linear at constant speed: increase the blending radius; the speed stays flat, the corners get rounder.
Program it

In the 6-axis sandbox you can record the three corners and export the program in RAPID or KRL: change fine into z10 and compare.

Key terms

  • linear motion (MoveL, LIN)
  • joint motion (MoveJ, PTP)
  • blending, zone
  • trapezoidal speed profile
The essentials
  • Linear (MoveL, LIN): straight sides, stop at each corner.
  • Joint (MoveJ, PTP): the joints move together, the path is curved, fastest for the motors.
  • Linear at constant speed (zone z10, C_DIS): straight sides, rounded corners, no stop: gluing.

One target, several postures: aspects and redundancy

20 min
You will learn

why the inverse model has 0, 1, several or infinitely many answers, the 8 aspects of a 6-axis arm such as the UR3, the infinity of postures of a redundant arm such as the KUKA LBR, and what the extra freedom is used for.

In one sentence

For a serial robot one posture gives one tool position, but one tool position can come from several postures (the aspects) — and from infinitely many when the robot has more joints than the task needs (redundancy).

4.1 How many answers?

SituationAnswers of the IGMExample
target outside the workspace0a point 2.5 m away for a 2 m arm
on the border, arm stretched1θ₂ = 0 for the 2-link arm
inside, as many joints as task coordinatesa finite number: the aspects2 for a 2-link arm, up to 8 for a 6R arm
more joints than task coordinatesinfinitely many7-axis arm, or 3R arm for a point of the plane

4.2 The 8 aspects of a 6-axis arm

For a classic 6R arm (UR3, ABB, KUKA, Fanuc), three binary choices multiply: shoulder left or right (2) × elbow up or down (2) × wrist flipped or not (2) = 8 aspects. For the UR3 and the target E = (300, 200, −250) mm (its base plane at z = −400 mm), solving the IGM gives 8 real postures; for other targets only 4 remain (some choices hit the joint limits or leave the workspace), and outside the workspace none.

The controller must choose

This is why an industrial robot target stores more than x, y, z and the orientation: ABB stores a configuration confdata, KUKA the S and T bits. Without them, the robot could reach the right point in a surprising posture — and hit the fence on the way.

4.3 Redundancy: infinitely many postures

The degree of redundancy is the number of joints minus the number of coordinates the task needs: r = n − m. The KUKA LBR (LWR) has 7 joints for a 6-coordinate task: r = 1 (try it on the 7-axis robot page). Holding the tool still, a single parameter remains free — often the “elbow angle”, the rotation of the elbow around the shoulder–wrist line. Worked example: count the redundancy
  • 3R planar arm, task = a point of the plane (x, y): r = 3 − 2 = 1. Task = point and orientation (x, y, φ): r = 3 − 3 = 0.
  • 6R arm used for spot welding, where the rotation about the electrode axis does not matter: m = 5, r = 1.
  • 6R arm used only to position a point (x, y, z): r = 3.

4.4 What is the extra freedom for?

  • to go around an obstacle with the elbow while the tool keeps working;
  • to stay away from the joint limits and from the singularities;
  • to choose the posture that resists best to a force, or that uses the least torque;
  • for a cobot, to let the operator push the elbow away by hand without moving the tool.

The redundancy lesson of the singularities course shows how the controller does this at the level of speeds, with the null space of the Jacobian.

Key terms

The essentials
  • IGM answers: 0 (outside), 1 (border), a finite number (aspects), infinitely many (redundancy).
  • 6R arm: up to 8 aspects = shoulder × elbow × wrist; the controller stores the configuration (confdata, S/T).
  • Degree of redundancy r = n − m; self-motion moves the arm, not the tool.

Parallel robots: definition and mobility

25 min
You will learn

what makes a robot parallel, why industry uses them (Delta, hexapods, five-bar), their strong and weak points, and how to count the degrees of freedom of a closed mechanism with Grübler’s formula — and when the formula lies.

In one sentence

A parallel robot holds its effector with several chains that all start from the base, so the motors stay on the base: light, stiff and fast, but with a smaller workspace and harder models.

5.1 Definitions

  • A parallel robot is made of several (closed) chains that support the effector, with the minimal number of actuators — as many as the degrees of freedom of the effector — and the effector has 0 degree of freedom when the motors are locked.
  • It is fully parallel when the number of chains equals the number of degrees of freedom of the effector, with one motor per chain (Delta: 3 chains, 3 dof; Gough-Stewart: 6 legs, 6 dof).
  • Joints are named by letters: R revolute (pivot), P prismatic (slider), U universal (cardan, 2 dof), S spherical (ball joint, 3 dof). The motorised one is underlined; “3-RPR” means three identical legs pivot–slider–pivot.
Serial robotParallel robot
Moving masshigh: each motor carries the next oneslow: motors on the base
Speed, accelerationmoderatevery high (Delta: 10 g, 200+ picks per minute)
Stiffness, accuracyerrors add up along the chainthe chains share the load: stiff, accurate
Workspacelargesmall compared to the size of the robot
ModelsDGM easy, IGM hardIGM easy, DGM hard
Singularitiesat the border and in a few known posturesalso inside the workspace, of a dangerous kind (lesson 6)

Where you meet them: Delta robots for packaging food and drugs; Gough-Stewart hexapods for flight simulators, telescope mirrors and precision positioning (try one); five-bar robots (DexTAR) for fast planar tasks; machine tools with parallel kinematics. The advanced course details the Delta and the Stewart platform.

5.2 Counting the mobility: Grübler’s formula

Each free rigid body has 3 degrees of freedom in the plane (6 in space). Each joint removes some of them and keeps dᵢ. With l rigid bodies including the base and the effector and n joints:

plane: m = 3 (l − n − 1) + Σ dᵢ    space: m = 6 (l − n − 1) + Σ dᵢ

Worked examples
  • Five-bar (plane): l = 5 bodies (base, 2 proximal arms, 2 distal arms — the effector is the joint between the distal arms), n = 5 pivots, Σdᵢ = 5. m = 3(5 − 5 − 1) + 5 = 2: two motors place the point (x, y).
  • Gough-Stewart 6-SPS (space): each leg = 2 bodies, so l = 6 × 2 + 2 = 14; n = 6 × 3 = 18; Σdᵢ = 6 × (3 + 1 + 3) = 42. m = 6(14 − 18 − 1) + 42 = 12 ≠ 6!
  • Why 12? Each leg, held by two ball joints, can spin about its own axis: 6 internal motions that move nothing. Replace the bottom ball joint by a universal joint (6-UPS): Σdᵢ = 6 × 6 = 36, m = 6.
When Grübler lies

The formula assumes that every constraint is independent. Two identical chains with superposed pivots give m = 3(3 − 3 − 1) + 3 = 0, yet the mechanism moves (m = 1): the second chain repeats the constraints of the first. Redundant constraints and internal motions make the formula wrong; always check with the geometry.

  • parallel robot
  • fully parallel
  • closed chain
  • mobility

  • Gough-Stewart platform, hexapod

The essentials
  • Parallel robot: several chains from the base hold the effector; as many actuators as dof; motors on the base.
  • Light, stiff, fast and accurate, but small workspace, hard DGM and singularities inside the workspace.
  • Grübler: m = 3(l − n − 1) + Σdᵢ (plane), 6(l − n − 1) + Σdᵢ (space); wrong with redundant constraints or internal motions.

Models of parallel robots: inverse, direct, kinematic, singular

30 min
You will learn

the inverse model chain by chain, the direct model by Newton’s method, the kinematic model A θ̇ + B Ẋ = 0 and its two matrices, the serial and parallel singularities, the five-bar and the 3-RPR solved step by step, and the design principles (Tripteron, spherical robots).

In one sentence

For a parallel robot, going from the effector to the motors is easy (each chain alone), going from the motors to the effector needs numerical methods, and the speeds obey A θ̇ + B Ẋ = 0: when A is singular a direction is lost, when B is singular the robot loses control.

Notation: θa the actuated joint variables, X the position (and orientation) parameters of the effector, W its velocity (linear and angular).

6.1 Inverse geometric model: θₐ = G(X)

Knowing X, every chain is attached at a known point of the effector: each chain becomes a small serial robot whose tool position is known. Solve its IGM alone — that is all. For the five-bar each chain is a 2-link arm; for a Gough-Stewart each leg length is simply the distance between its two ball joints.

Worked example: IGM of the five-bar

Motors 0.3 m apart: O₁₁ = (−0.15, 0), O₁₂ = (0.15, 0); proximal arms 0.3 m, distal arms 0.4 m. Target O₃ = (0, 0.4).

  1. Chain 1: distance O₁₁O₃ = √(0.15² + 0.4²) = 0.427 m, direction atan2(0.4, 0.15) = 69.4°.
  2. Law of cosines for the angle at the motor: cos α = (0.3² + 0.427² − 0.4²) / (2 × 0.3 × 0.427) = 0.439, α = 64.0°.
  3. Elbow outwards: θ₁₁ = 69.4° + 64.0° = 133.4°. By symmetry θ₁₂ = 180° − 133.4° = 46.6°. Each chain had 2 choices: 2 × 2 = 4 working modes.

6.2 Direct geometric model: X = H(θₐ), by Newton

Going the other way has no general solution: the chains must close, which gives coupled nonlinear equations (up to 40 solutions for a general Gough-Stewart!). In practice we use the IGM and the Jacobian in Newton’s method: start from a guess X₀ (the last known position), compare the motor angles it would need with the real ones, and correct:

Xk+1 = Xk + J (θₐ − G(Xk))    with J = ∂H / ∂θₐ

At 1 kHz the robot barely moves between two cycles, so the last position is an excellent guess: 2 or 3 iterations are enough, and Newton stays on the right one of the many solutions.

6.3 Kinematic model: A θ̇ + B Ẋ = 0

  1. For each chain i, write the effector velocity as for a serial robot, with all its joints, active and passive: W = Jᵢ θ̇ᵢ.
  2. Project these equations on well-chosen directions to eliminate the passive joint speeds, keeping one equation per actuated variable: Pᵢ W = Pᵢ Jᵢ θ̇ᵢ = −Aᵢ θ̇ₐᵢ.
  3. Stack all the chains: B W = −A θ̇ₐ, hence Ẋ = J θ̇ₐ with J = −B⁻¹ A.
The five-bar (DexTAR)

The speed of O₃ through chain j is θ̇₁ⱼ z × O₁ⱼO₃ + θ̇₂ⱼ z × O₂ⱼO₃. The passive speed θ̇₂ⱼ multiplies a vector perpendicular to the distal arm, so projecting on the direction x₂ⱼ of the distal arm removes it: x₂ⱼ · V = θ̇₁ⱼ x₂ⱼ · (z × O₁ⱼO₃). Two chains, two lines:

[x₂₁ᵀ ; x₂₂ᵀ] (vₓ, v_y)ᵀ = diag(x₂₁ · (z × O₁₁O₃), x₂₂ · (z × O₁₂O₃)) (θ̇₁₁, θ̇₁₂)ᵀ

B is made of the directions of the distal arms; A is diagonal.

The 3-RPR

Each leg j has a direction uⱼ and an actuated length r₂ⱼ. Multiplying the velocity twist of the platform by the reciprocal twist of the leg (the force along uⱼ) removes the passive pivots: ṙ₂ⱼ = uⱼ · v + uⱼ · (z × CAⱼ) θ̇, where C is the centre of the platform and Aⱼ the end of the leg. Three legs, three lines: the Jacobian of the 3-RPR.

6.4 The two kinds of singularity

Serial singularity: det A = 0Parallel singularity: det B = 0
Geometry (five-bar)a chain stretched or folded (proximal and distal arms aligned)the two distal arms aligned
What happensthe effector cannot move in one direction: a loss of mobility, usually at the border of the workspacemotors locked, the effector can still move: a gain of mobility, uncontrolled, often inside the workspace
Forcesthe structure resists by itself in that directionthe motor torques needed to resist a force grow without bound: danger

Designers keep parallel singularities out of the useful workspace, and trajectories never cross them. The DexTAR robot can change working mode on purpose, but only along carefully planned paths.

6.5 Design principles

  • The possible speeds of the effector are the intersection of the possible speeds of every chain; the forces it can apply or resist are the union of the forces of every chain.
  • So every chain must at least allow the motions wanted for the effector (a 6-dof platform needs 6-dof chains), and anything one chain blocks is blocked for the effector.
  • Tripteron: each of its three chains blocks two rotations (it transmits two torques); together they block the three rotations: a pure translator x, y, z, with a constant Jacobian.
  • Spherical robots: one chain ending with a ball joint at a fixed point blocks the three translations; the other chains only orient (camera heads, wrists).

The essentials
  • IGM θₐ = G(X): each chain alone. DGM X = H(θₐ): Newton X ← X + J(θₐ − G(X)).
  • Kinematics: A θ̇ + B Ẋ = 0, J = −B⁻¹A; project each chain to eliminate its passive joints.
  • det A = 0: serial singularity, a direction lost. det B = 0: parallel singularity, uncontrolled motion — avoid!

Five-bar, motor at (−0.15, 0), arms 0.3 and 0.4 m, target (0, 0.4). Distance motor–target (m)?

Solution

√(0.15² + 0.4²) ≈ 0.427 m.

Level 3 · Professional2

Same: the motor angle with the elbow outwards (°)?

Solution

69.4° + acos(0.439) = 69.4° + 64.0° = 133.4°.

Level 1 · Beginner3

The two distal arms of a five-bar are aligned. Which singularity?

  1. Parallel (det B = 0)
  2. Serial (det A = 0)

Solution

B is made of the distal arm directions: aligned arms make it singular.

Check yourself

  1. Why is the IGM of a parallel robot easy?

    Knowing the effector, each chain is a small serial robot with a known tool position: solved alone.

  2. Write the Newton update of the DGM.

    X_{k+1} = X_k + J (θa − G(X_k)), J the Jacobian, G the IGM.

  3. What happens at a parallel singularity (det B = 0)?

    With the motors locked the effector can still move; the torques needed to resist a force become infinite.
